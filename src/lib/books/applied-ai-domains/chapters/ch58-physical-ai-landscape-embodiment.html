Everything in the first three books of this series lives on a screen. A model reads tokens or pixels from a file, produces a prediction or a completion, and the worst-case failure is a wrong answer in a chat window or a bad recommendation in a dashboard. Physical AI breaks that assumption in a specific, mechanical way: the model’s output now moves something. A wrong prediction doesn’t just sit there. It drives a motor, steers a wheel, closes a gripper. This chapter exists to make that difference concrete before the rest of this track touches any modelling technique, because the difference changes what “correct” even means.

58.1 Motivation

None of what follows makes physical AI a different kind of machine learning. The models met later in this track, world models, vision-language-action models, RL-trained policies, are built from the same primitives as everything in Books 1-3: neural networks, gradient descent, attention. What’s different is the system around the model: what it senses, how fast it has to act, and what happens when it’s wrong. A recommendation model that’s late by 200ms produces a stale suggestion; a balance controller that’s late by 200ms has already fallen over. That difference in consequence, and in timing budget, is the organizing idea of this entire track, and this chapter lays out its vocabulary and its central loop before any of the later, more sophisticated modules build on it.

58.2.2 The Sense-Plan-Act Loop

Every physical AI system, no matter how sophisticated, is an instance of one loop: sense, turn raw sensor signal into a useful representation of the world (Chapter 59, Perception); plan, decide what to do next given that representation (Chapters 60-61, World Models and Vision-Language-Action Models, both of which are, in different ways, methods for turning “here’s what I sense” into “here’s what should happen next”); act, turn a decision into motor commands (Chapter 63, Learning Robot Policies, where the thing that actually produces actions gets trained). Chapters 62 (Simulation), 64 (Edge Compute), and 65 (Deployment & Safety) aren’t stages of the loop itself. They’re about where the loop gets practiced before touching hardware (62), what it physically runs on (64), and how it’s operated responsibly once live (65, extending Book 2’s Responsible AI material into a domain where the stakes are physical).

58.2.3 Degrees of Freedom, Sensors, and Actuators

A robot’s degrees of freedom (DOF) is the number of independent ways it can move: a single rotating joint is 1 DOF, a human arm (shoulder, elbow, wrist) has roughly 7. More DOF means more dexterity and a larger workspace (the reachable volume), but also a harder control and planning problem: every DOF is another number a policy has to get right, simultaneously, many times per second.

Sensors turn the physical world into numbers:

Sensor Measures Typical use
Camera (RGB) Visible light, 2D image Object recognition, scene understanding
Depth camera (RGB-D) Distance per pixel 3D scene reconstruction, grasping
LiDAR Time-of-flight point cloud Mapping, obstacle detection at range
IMU (inertial measurement unit) Acceleration + angular velocity Orientation, balance, dead-reckoning
Joint encoder Angle or position of a joint Proprioception: knowing the robot’s own pose
Force/torque sensor Contact force Safe manipulation, detecting collisions

Actuators turn numbers back into motion: motors, servos, hydraulic/pneumatic cylinders, grippers. Every actuator has limits (maximum torque, maximum speed, minimum achievable increment), and part of the sim-to-real gap is that simulated actuators are usually idealised (instant, unlimited, noiseless) in ways real ones never are.

58.2.4 Open-Loop vs. Closed-Loop Control

Open-loop control issues commands without checking the result: “drive forward at 1 m/s for 3 seconds.” It’s simple and requires no sensing during execution, but any error (a slightly uneven floor, a slightly under-powered motor) accumulates with nothing to correct it. This is dead-reckoning, and it drifts.

Closed-loop control measures the actual outcome and corrects for the difference between where the system is and where it should be, continuously:

error=target−measuredcommand=f(error)\text{error} = \text{target} - \text{measured} \qquad \text{command} = f(\text{error})error=target−measuredcommand=f(error)

This chapter’s tutorial implements the simplest and most common version of fff: a PID controller (Proportional-Integral-Derivative), which computes a command from the current error, the accumulated error over time, and the rate of change of the error:

Almost every real-world closed-loop controller, from a robot arm’s joint controller to a thermostat, is either literally a PID controller or a refinement of one.

58.2.5 Why Control Runs at Fixed, High Frequency

A chatbot can take 2 seconds to respond and the user barely notices. A balance controller for a two-wheeled robot that takes 2 seconds to respond has already fallen over. Physical control loops typically run at a fixed frequency, commonly 50-1000 Hz depending on the task, for three reasons. First, the physical world doesn’t pause while a model thinks; a late correction is a wrong correction. Second, closed-loop stability analysis generally assumes a fixed, known control-loop period. An inconsistent loop rate can make an otherwise-stable controller oscillate or diverge. Third, downstream components, a learned policy (Chapter 63), a VLA model (Chapter 61), have to fit their inference time inside this loop’s budget, which is exactly why Chapter 64 (Edge Compute) cares so much about inference latency and why models get compressed (quantised, pruned, distilled) to run on-device rather than round-tripping to the cloud.

Task category Typical control loop frequency
High-level planning / task selection 1-10 Hz
Navigation / path following 10-50 Hz
Manipulator joint control 100-1000 Hz
Balance / legged locomotion 200-1000+ Hz

58.2.6 The Sim-to-Real Gap

Training or testing directly on physical hardware is slow, expensive, and, critically, unsafe when the policy is still bad. So training happens in simulation instead. But a simulator is a model of physics, not physics, and it’s always wrong in some way: friction coefficients are approximate, sensor noise is idealised, contact dynamics are notoriously hard to simulate accurately. A policy that performs perfectly in simulation can fail on the first real trial because it has implicitly learned to exploit some quirk of the simulator that doesn’t exist in reality. Domain randomization, deliberately varying simulated physics, lighting, and textures during training so the policy can’t overfit to any one simulated world, is the primary mitigation, covered in full in Chapter 62.

58.3 Trade-offs and Limits

More DOF vs. controllability. A robot with more degrees of freedom can do more, but every additional DOF adds another dimension a planner or a learned policy has to get right simultaneously. Simple tasks are often best served by the simplest robot that can do them: a 1-2 DOF gripper or pan-tilt mount is fine for a single constrained motion and is cheapest to build and control; a 6-DOF industrial arm is the standard for general manipulation because 6 is exactly enough to reach any position and orientation in 3D space (3 for position, 3 for orientation); 7+ DOF (“redundant”) arms add flexibility to avoid obstacles or awkward configurations for the same end-effector pose, at the cost of infinitely many joint solutions for one target pose.

Simulation fidelity vs. training speed. A highly accurate simulator is slow to run, which limits how many training episodes are affordable. A fast, simplified simulator allows more training, but risks a wider sim-to-real gap. Chapter 62 covers this trade-off directly.

Edge inference vs. model capability. The biggest, most capable model isn’t an option if it can’t run inside the control loop’s latency budget on the hardware being shipped. Chapter 64 covers the compression techniques that make this trade-off less painful.

58.4 Procedures

Procedure 58.1 (Simulate a Physical Plant with Friction and a Constant Disturbance). When to use: building any closed-loop control example, real or for teaching, where the system needs sustained control effort to hold a position away from equilibrium.

Procedure 58.2 (Implement and Tune a PID Controller). When to use: any closed-loop system, real or simulated, that needs kp, ki, kd chosen.

Manual tuning sequence: set ki=0, kd=0; increase kp until the system responds quickly but starts to oscillate, then back off to roughly 50-70% of that value. Add kd gradually. It damps oscillation and reduces overshoot, but too much amplifies sensor noise (the derivative of a noisy measurement is more noise). Add ki only if there’s a persistent steady-state error, and increase it slowly. Too much causes slow oscillation (“integral windup”) that’s hard to damp back out. Procedure 58.4 (Reason About Sensor Selection). When to use: early in scoping a physical AI system, before committing to a hardware bill of materials. For each sensor candidate, ask: what does it directly measure (a camera measures light, not distance; depth is inferred); what’s its update rate (an IMU might run at 1000 Hz, a LiDAR scan might take 50-100ms); what’s its noise characteristic (camera-based depth is noisier at range than dedicated depth sensors); what does it cost in dollars and in compute to process (raw LiDAR point clouds are expensive to process in real time); and does it work in the deployment environment (cameras struggle in low light/glare, some LiDAR struggles outdoors in direct sun). Common mistake: picking the sensor with the best headline spec without checking it can be processed fast enough to fit inside the control loop’s latency budget.

Full PID settles at position 5.0016 (error -0.0016); P-only settles at 4.6142 (error 0.3857). The P-only offset is not a tuning mistake. It’s structural: at equilibrium the proportional term kp * error has to exactly balance the bias force, and a smaller error would mean less counter-force, which the bias force would immediately overcome. Only the integral term, which keeps accumulating as long as any error persists, can drive that error to zero.

The second comparison is the chapter’s central argument. An open-loop controller computes one fixed force at the start (assuming friction=0.3, no bias force) and applies it blindly for the whole run; a closed-loop PID measures and corrects every step. Results: open-loop with a matching model reaches 4.171 (target 5.0), even a correct model only gets close, because friction depends on velocity, which changes throughout the run, so a single fixed force was always an approximation. Open-loop with the real friction 4x higher than assumed reaches only 1.199. The controller has no way to notice or correct, because it never measures anything. 58.9 Mental Model

Physical AI is digital AI plus a clock and a body: the same learned components, wrapped in a sense-plan-act loop that has to run fast enough and correct for a world that never matches the model exactly. Closed-loop beats open-loop not because it’s smarter, but because it trades “predict the world correctly” for the much easier “measure the error and correct it.” That trade is the single idea underneath every chapter that follows in this track.
