Chapter 58 named the sense-plan-act loop and left “sense” as a placeholder, a box labelled “turn raw sensor signal into a useful representation of the world.” This chapter opens that box. A robot’s perception stack has to answer four increasingly spatial questions: what’s in the scene, how far away is it, where am I relative to a map of it, and how confident should I be given that every sensor answering these questions is individually flawed. Detection, depth estimation, SLAM, and sensor fusion are the four classical answers to those four questions, and every later chapter in this track, world models, vision-language-action models, learned policies, takes some version of their output as an input.

59.1 Motivation

If you’ve worked through the transformer and neural network material in Book 2, you already know how to classify an image: pixels in, a label out. Robotic perception uses the same underlying building blocks (convolutions, attention, learned representations) but the job differs in three ways that matter in practice. First, it has to run in real time, on a budget: a cloud API call that takes 800ms is a minor annoyance in a chatbot, but in a robot moving at 1 m/s, 800ms of latency is 80cm of travel with no updated picture of the world. Chapter 58’s control-loop-frequency argument applies just as much to perception as to control, and Chapter 64 (Edge Compute) covers the on-device budget this forces. Second, it has to understand 3D space, not just assign 2D labels: “there’s a cup in this image” is not enough to pick the cup up. A gripper needs to know where, in 3D, relative to itself, which is what depth estimation and point clouds are for. Third, a wrong perception becomes a physical action: a mislabelled photo is embarrassing, but a robot that mistakes a person’s hand for empty space is dangerous. Perception for physical AI needs calibrated uncertainty, not just a confident top-1 label, a theme Chapter 65 (Deployment, Fleet Monitoring & Safety) returns to at the system level.

59.2 Exposition

59.2.1 Detection, Semantic Segmentation, and Instance Segmentation

These three are often confused because they all draw boxes or outlines on an image, but they answer progressively narrower, and progressively more expensive, questions.

Task Output Answers Relative compute cost
Object detection Bounding boxes + class labels “Is X present, roughly where?” Low
Semantic segmentation Per-pixel class label “Which pixels are class X?” (no notion of individual objects) Medium
Instance segmentation Per-pixel class label + instance ID “Which pixels belong to this specific object?” High

Object detection is cheap and fast and good enough when the downstream task only needs to know that something is there: can the arm reach this region? Semantic segmentation labels every pixel but can’t tell two overlapping cups apart; they’re just one “cup” region. Instance segmentation, the most expensive of the three, is what’s needed when a robot has to pick up this specific cup, not just recognise that cups exist. The rule of thumb: choose the cheapest task that answers the actual downstream question. Reaching for instance segmentation by default because it’s “the most information” wastes compute and latency that Chapter 64’s edge budget will not forgive.

59.2.2 Depth Estimation

A 2D detection alone doesn’t tell a gripper how far away an object is. There are three common ways to recover depth, in increasing order of hardware cost and decreasing order of ambiguity.

Method Accuracy Hardware cost Key weakness
Monocular (learned) Low-medium, scale-ambiguous Lowest: one camera No real geometric signal, only a learned prior; a photo of a photo can fool it
Stereo Medium-high, metric Medium: two calibrated cameras Needs scene texture; fails on blank or reflective surfaces
LiDAR Highest, metric, lighting-independent Highest: dedicated sensor Sparse; expensive; heavier and more power-hungry

Monocular depth estimation uses a learned model to predict depth from a single 2D image, relying on priors like “objects higher in the frame and smaller are usually farther away.” It’s the cheapest option but fundamentally ambiguous, since it has no real geometric signal to fall back on. Stereo depth uses two cameras a known baseline distance apart: near objects shift more between the two views than far objects, and that shift, disparity, converts directly to metric depth:

depth=baseline×focal_lengthdisparity\text{depth} = \frac{\text{baseline} \times \text{focal\_length}}{\text{disparity}}depth=disparitybaseline×focal_length​

Stereo is geometrically grounded rather than learned, but needs enough texture in the scene for the two views to be matched confidently, and needs the cameras calibrated and rectified first. Skipping rectification is the single most common cause of a stereo depth map looking smeared or wrong. LiDAR measures depth directly by timing a reflected laser pulse, independent of image texture or lighting, and is usually the source of “ground truth” depth used to train or validate the other two methods, at the cost of weight, power, and price.

59.2.3 Point Clouds, SLAM, and Loop Closure

A point cloud is simply a set of 3D points, the natural output of LiDAR, or of triangulating a stereo or depth-camera image. On its own it’s a snapshot of what the robot can currently see. SLAM (Simultaneous Localization and Mapping) is the problem of building a consistent map from a sequence of these snapshots while simultaneously tracking the robot’s position within that map, and the two halves depend on each other: placing a new scan on the map correctly requires knowing where the robot is, and knowing where the robot is requires the map.

The hard part of SLAM is loop closure: recognising “I’ve been in this exact spot before” after wandering away and coming back, and correcting the accumulated drift once that’s recognised. That recognition-and-correction step is a point cloud alignment problem: given a new scan and a candidate match from the existing map, find the rigid transform (rotation plus translation) that best aligns them. ICP (Iterative Closest Point) is the classic algorithm for exactly this, and it runs in four steps repeated to convergence: pair each point in the source cloud with its current nearest neighbour in the target cloud; solve for the single rigid transform that best explains all those pairings (via SVD, the Kabsch algorithm); apply that transform to the whole source cloud; and stop once the mean point-to-point error stops improving. This is exactly what a SLAM system runs at loop closure: “does this new scan match somewhere already mapped, and if so, what’s the transform?” And it’s also the reason odometry, pose graph, and landmark show up as SLAM vocabulary: odometry (from wheel encoders or an IMU) is accurate short-term but drifts, and loop closure is precisely the correction that keeps that drift from accumulating forever.

59.2.4 Sensor Fusion and the Kalman Filter

No single sensor is good at everything:

Sensor Strength Weakness
Camera Rich semantic detail (what, what colour) No direct depth; fails in low light
LiDAR Accurate, lighting-independent depth Sparse, expensive, no colour/texture
IMU Fast, always-available motion estimate Drifts (accumulates error) with no external correction
Wheel odometry Cheap, always-available Wrong on slip (wheels spin, robot doesn’t move)

Sensor fusion combines multiple noisy, individually-flawed estimates into one that’s better than any single input, not by picking the “best” sensor, but by weighting each sensor’s contribution by how much it should currently be trusted. The Kalman filter is the standard tool: it maintains a running estimate xxx and its uncertainty (variance) PPP, and alternates two steps. Predict projects the estimate forward using a motion model, and uncertainty always grows during this step since nothing new has been observed:

QQQ is process noise (how much uncertainty accumulates per predict step), RRR is observation noise (how noisy the sensor reading is), and KKK, the Kalman gain, is the weighting applied to the new observation, always in [0,1][0,1][0,1]: 0 means ignore the observation entirely, 1 means trust it completely. The noisier sensor gets less say. Because the two error sources are independent, combining them reduces overall uncertainty even though neither sensor individually became more accurate. That’s the entire case for fusion over picking the single best sensor.

59.3 Trade-offs and Limits

Classical vs. learned perception. Thresholding, block-matching stereo, ICP, and static-gain Kalman fusion are all classical algorithms, no training data, no gradient descent, deliberately chosen so the mechanics stay visible and the pipeline runs anywhere with no downloads. Production systems typically replace the detection/segmentation and monocular-depth pieces with learned models (trained the way Book 2, Chapter 28, Intro to Neural Networks, and Chapter 29, Transformers, cover), because learned models generalise to open-world scenes that hand-crafted thresholds cannot. Point cloud alignment (ICP) and sensor fusion (Kalman and particle filters), by contrast, are still classical algorithms in most production SLAM stacks. They’re well understood, need no training data, and give explicit uncertainty estimates that learned black boxes often don’t.

Information vs. compute, at every layer. Detection is cheaper than semantic segmentation is cheaper than instance segmentation; monocular depth is cheaper than stereo is cheaper than LiDAR. Each “better” option costs more compute, more hardware, or both, and the real-time constraint from Chapter 58 means the cheapest option that answers the actual downstream question usually wins. Chapter 64 (Edge Compute) covers exactly how that trade-off gets made once the question is “what actually fits on this device.”

A Kalman filter is only as good as its variance estimates. Feeding the filter a fixed observation variance that doesn’t reflect reality (treating a camera estimate as equally trustworthy in daylight and near-total darkness, for instance) is worse than not fusing at all, because a confidently-wrong variance lets a bad sensor drag down a good one rather than being correctly discounted.

59.4 Procedures

Procedure 59.1 (Classical Detection via Thresholding and Contours). When to use: a first perception pass on any scene where the objects of interest are visually distinct from the background. A starting point before reaching for a learned detector.

This is the same three-stage pattern, segment, extract boundary, classify, that a learned detector performs internally, just with hand-crafted rather than learned features. Common mistake: running this on a raw, un-thresholded image; findContours needs a binary image, and the threshold value (100 here) has to be chosen relative to the actual foreground/background separation in the scene, not copied from a different dataset.

Procedure 59.2 (Recover Metric Depth from a Stereo Pair). When to use: two calibrated, synchronised cameras a known distance apart are available and metric depth is needed.

Cameras must be calibrated (lens distortion corrected) and rectified (rotated so corresponding rows in both images line up) before stereo matching works at all. cv2.stereoCalibrate / cv2.stereoRectify, using a checkerboard pattern, handle this. Common mistake: using the millimetre focal length off a lens spec sheet directly instead of the calibrated pixel focal length (focal_px) that comes out of calibration. The formula needs the latter.

Procedure 59.3 (Align Two Point Clouds with ICP). When to use: aligning a new sensor scan against a previous one or an existing map. The core operation behind SLAM loop closure.

Before running ICP at any real scale, downsample the point cloud first. Brute-force nearest-neighbour search, as above, is O(n×m) per iteration, and voxel downsampling (keeping one representative point per fixed-size cube) is often the difference between real-time and unusable. Common mistake: starting ICP from an identity transform on clouds that don’t overlap well or start far apart. It converges to the wrong local alignment. Seed it with an odometry-based initial guess instead.

Procedure 59.4 (Fuse Two Independent Estimates with a Kalman Filter). When to use: two or more independent, noisy estimates of the same quantity (position, velocity, orientation) exist and a single better estimate is needed.

Common mistake: trusting a sensor’s nominal spec-sheet noise figure instead of the variance it actually exhibits in the deployment environment. Re-estimate each sensor’s true noise variance from held-out calibration data rather than guessing.

Procedure 59.5 (Benchmark a Perception Model’s Latency Before Deployment). When to use: before any detector, segmentation, or depth model goes onto physical hardware. Accuracy alone is not a deployment decision.

Check p99, not just mean or p50. A robot doesn’t get to ignore its slowest 1% of perception cycles, and that’s exactly when it’s most likely to make a bad decision under stale information. A model that’s 2% more accurate but 3x slower at p99 is often the worse choice once it’s inside Chapter 58’s control loop.

59.9 Mental Model

Detection tells you what. Depth tells you how far. Point clouds and SLAM tell you where, on a map, and where you are on it. Fusion tells you how confident to be, by combining every source of evidence available. None of the four techniques in this chapter is smarter than the others. Each is the cheapest tool that answers one narrower, more spatially grounded question than the last, and a working perception stack is just the smallest combination of them that answers the questions the downstream task actually asks.
