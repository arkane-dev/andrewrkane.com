Chapter 59 turns raw sensor data into a structured picture of what’s out there right now. That’s necessary but not sufficient for acting in the world. A robot also needs some way to answer “if I do X, what will the world look like next?” without actually doing X on real, expensive, breakable hardware first. A world model is exactly that: a learned model of how the world (or the robot’s observations of it) evolves in response to actions, used to predict, imagine, or plan ahead of time. It’s the difference between a robot that can only react to what it currently senses and one that can ask “what if” before committing to a motion.

60.1 Motivation

There are three broad families of world model. Pixel-space generative models predict the next frame, pixel by pixel, the world-model equivalent of a text-generation LLM, but for video; most of a frame’s pixels are irrelevant, unpredictable detail, and a model that spends capacity predicting those wastes effort that should go toward predicting what actually matters. Latent/representation predictors (the JEPA family) predict the next embedding instead of the next frame. Explicit dynamics models predict the next state directly, in a hand-defined state space. Worked Example 60 below builds and measures a real JEPA-style model, including the failure mode its central architectural trick exists to prevent.

60.2 Exposition

60.2.1 Latent-Space Predictive Models: the JEPA Family

JEPA (Joint Embedding Predictive Architecture) predicts an embedding, the output of a learned encoder, instead of a masked or future patch’s raw pixels, using a second, separately-updated encoder to produce the prediction target. This sidesteps the pixel-noise problem directly: an embedding can, and should, discard irrelevant texture/lighting noise while keeping the structure that actually matters, so the prediction task only has to get the meaningful part right. I-JEPA (Image JEPA) does this for still images: mask out several patches of an image, and predict their embeddings from the visible patches’ embeddings. V-JEPA (Video JEPA) extends this temporally: mask out a spatio-temporal block of a video clip, forcing the model to learn something about how scenes change over time, not just what a single frame looks like. The architecture has three pieces: a context encoder (embeds visible patches/frames, the only network receiving gradients directly), a target encoder (embeds masked patches to produce prediction targets, structurally identical to the context encoder but updated as an exponential moving average, EMA, of it, with no gradient of its own), and a predictor (given the context encoder’s output plus positional information about where the masked region is, predicts what the target encoder would output there).

60.2.2 Why the EMA Target Encoder Isn’t Optional

If the target encoder were just the same, freely-trainable network as the context encoder, the training objective has a trivial solution that has nothing to do with prediction: collapse every input to nearly the same output embedding. Then any prediction is “correct” by construction, because there’s nothing left to distinguish. Loss goes to near zero, and the model has learned nothing. This is representation collapse, the central engineering problem every self-supervised joint-embedding method has to solve. JEPA and BYOL use an EMA target encoder that updates slowly and receives no gradient. The target the predictor chases can’t collapse in lockstep with the context encoder, because it isn’t being trained to make the loss easy. SimSiam uses stop-gradient on one branch plus an architectural asymmetry. VICReg uses no separate target encoder at all, instead adding an explicit regularisation term that directly penalises low variance across the batch, making collapse costly rather than architecturally impossible. Worked Example 60 doesn’t just describe this. It removes the EMA mechanism and measures collapse happening directly.

60.2.3 Explicit Dynamics Models

The classical alternative, from model-based reinforcement learning: define a state space by hand (position, velocity, joint angles), and train a model to predict next_state = f(state, action) directly. This is what planning via rollouts uses: simulate several candidate action sequences forward through the learned dynamics model, score each resulting trajectory, and take the best one, all without touching real hardware. It’s more interpretable and sample-efficient than learning from raw pixels, but only works when a good, low-dimensional state space can be defined by hand, exactly the problem perception and JEPA-style representation learning exist to solve when a hand-designed state space isn’t available or isn’t rich enough.

60.3 Trade-offs and Limits

Pixel-space models produce genuinely useful outputs on their own (a predicted frame can be looked at directly) which latent-space embeddings don’t give without an additional decoder or probe, but pixel-space models pay for that with far more compute spent on unpredictable detail. Latent-space models are more sample- and compute-efficient for downstream tasks, but their embeddings are opaque without an additional decoder or probe. A hand-designed state space is interpretable and can be very sample-efficient, but only scales to problems simple enough to define by hand; learned representations scale to raw sensory input at the cost of interpretability. The EMA momentum itself is a trade-off: too low (target encoder updates too fast) risks reintroducing collapse, since the target starts moving in lockstep with the online network again; too high (target barely updates) slows learning, since the prediction target is stale relative to what the context encoder currently knows.

60.4 Procedures

Procedure 60.1 (Implement a Masking Scheme for Patch-Based Prediction). When to use: building the training loop for any JEPA-style masked-prediction model.

Common mistake: masking too little makes the task trivial (the predictor can nearly copy adjacent visible patches); masking too much leaves the predictor with no useful context to condition on. Both reduce how much the encoder is forced to learn.

Procedure 60.2 (Implement an EMA Target Encoder Update). When to use: any joint-embedding model (JEPA, BYOL-style) needing a target network that updates without receiving its own gradients.

Typical momentum values: 0.99–0.999 for models trained many thousands of steps; a short toy run can use a fixed value around 0.99–0.996. Too low and the target chases the online network too closely, risking collapse; too high and the target barely moves, slowing learning.

Procedure 60.3 (Check for Representation Collapse). When to use: a joint-embedding model’s loss looks suspiciously good, very low, very fast, which is the first symptom to check, not celebrate.

60.5 Worked Example

A minimal JEPA trained from scratch on synthetic images for 60 epochs:

Epoch Loss
0 0.00414
10 0.00101
30 0.00134
50 0.00101
59 (final) 0.00083

Loss drops steeply in the first few epochs then plateaus. Most of the easy structure is picked up fast. Before trusting this as “real prediction,” Procedure 60.3’s spirit demands a sharper check than the loss curve alone: does the predictor actually use its input, or has it found a trivial shortcut (always predicting the batch mean)?

Predictor MSE
Real, input-conditioned predictor 0.00080
“Always predict the mean” baseline 0.03201

The real predictor beats the trivial mean baseline by 40.1x, genuine, input-conditioned prediction, not a dataset-average shortcut hiding behind a low-looking loss number.

A linear probe (Exposition’s “sample efficiency through selective prediction” claim, tested directly) checks whether the learned embedding supports a downstream task, shape classification, that was never an explicit training target:

Encoder Linear probe accuracy (5 classes, chance = 0.20)
Trained JEPA encoder 0.488
Random-init encoder 0.436

The trained encoder beats both chance and a random-init baseline, though modestly on this toy-scale task. Real signal, not a dramatic result, and reported honestly as such rather than oversold.

Finally, the EMA mechanism (Procedure 60.2) is deliberately removed to watch representation collapse happen directly:

Run Final loss Embedding std
Healthy (with EMA) 0.000833 0.00540
Collapsed (no EMA) 0.000003 0.00039

The collapsed run’s loss is lower, 0.000003 versus 0.000833, which looks like a better result if only the loss curve is watched. But its embedding spread is roughly 14x smaller: every input is being mapped to nearly the same point, exactly Exposition’s predicted trivial solution. A loss curve alone cannot tell these two outcomes apart; Procedure 60.3’s variance check is what distinguishes “the model learned to predict well” from “the model learned to stop saying anything at all.”

AWS SageMaker Notes

World-model training (JEPA-style or otherwise) is a standard PyTorch training loop from SageMaker’s perspective. A PyTorch estimator on a ml.g5/ml.p4d instance runs Procedures 60.1-60.2 unmodified. The one operational addition worth building in for this specific architecture: log the embedding-variance health check (Procedure 60.3) as a custom CloudWatch metric alongside the training loss, since, as this chapter’s Worked Example demonstrates directly, a training dashboard showing only loss would flag the collapsed run as the better one.

60.6 History and Further Reading

Assran et al.’s “Self-Supervised Learning from Images with a Joint-Embedding Predictive Architecture” (Assran et al. 2023) is the original I-JEPA paper this chapter’s tutorial is a toy version of. Bardes et al.’s “V-JEPA” (Bardes et al. 2024) extends the same idea temporally. Grill et al.’s “Bootstrap Your Own Latent” (BYOL) (Grill et al. 2020) is the original source of the EMA-target-encoder trick. Bardes, Ponce & LeCun’s “VICReg” (Bardes et al. 2022) is the explicit-regularisation alternative to an EMA target encoder. Book 1’s Information Theory chapter provides the entropy/compression argument for why latent-space prediction beats pixel-space prediction.

60.7 How This Connects

This chapter builds on Chapter 59 (a world model predicts the next structured representation, which presupposes a structured representation of the current observation already exists) and Book 1’s Information Theory chapter (the “don’t waste capacity modelling irrelevant entropy” argument for latent-space prediction is a direct application of that chapter’s entropy/compression framing). It enables Chapter 61, which sometimes trains with world-model-style auxiliary objectives alongside action prediction, and Chapter 63, where model-based planning uses a learned world model to evaluate candidate actions without touching hardware. It’s often confused with video generation models built to produce visually pleasing or realistic output (a product goal) versus world models built to be a useful, compact, predictive representation for planning (an engineering goal). The two overlap in technique but not in objective. It’s also easily confused with plain autoencoders: an autoencoder’s target is its own input; a JEPA’s target is a different, separately-encoded view of a related input. The asymmetry between context and target is the whole point, and Worked Example 60’s collapse experiment shows exactly what happens when that asymmetry is removed.

60.9 Mental Model

A world model answers one question: if the world currently looks like this, what happens next? Pixel-space models answer it in exhaustive, expensive detail. JEPA-style latent models answer it in a compressed, learned vocabulary that, if trained well, keeps what matters and discards what doesn’t. The entire architectural apparatus of a separate, slowly-updating target encoder exists for one reason: to stop the model from cheating its way to a low loss by refusing to say anything at all. This chapter’s Worked Example made that cheating visible and measurable: a 0.000003 loss that represents nothing learned, right next to a 0.000833 loss that represents real, input-conditioned, 40x-better-than-baseline prediction.
