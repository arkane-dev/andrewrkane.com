Chapter 58 named the sim-to-real gap without explaining how anyone actually deals with it. This chapter does. Training or testing a policy directly on real hardware is slow (one trial at a time, real-time only), expensive (real robots break), and unsafe (a bad policy can hurt itself, its surroundings, or a person while it’s still learning). Simulation solves all three: thousands of trials can run in parallel, faster than real time, with zero physical risk. The cost is that a simulator is a model of physics, not physics, and every place that model is wrong is a place a policy can learn to exploit a quirk that doesn’t exist in reality.

62.1 Motivation

Three properties trade off against each other in any robotics simulator: physics accuracy (how faithfully contact dynamics, friction, and articulated-body motion match reality; contact dynamics are notoriously hard to simulate accurately, and a lot of the sim-to-real gap lives here), rendering fidelity (how photorealistic the visual output is; matters enormously for vision-based policies, not at all for policies that only ever see joint angles and velocities), and speed/parallelism (how many simulated steps per second, and critically, how many environment instances can run simultaneously; a simulator that can only run one environment at a time, even quickly, still bottlenecks training on wall-clock time). No simulator maximises all three simultaneously, and which one to reach for depends on which property a specific task actually needs. Worked Example 62 below measures directly what the core mechanism for narrowing the sim-to-real gap, domain randomization, is worth, numerically, not just conceptually.

62.2 Exposition

62.2.1 Isaac Sim vs. Isaac Lab

These are two different layers, often confused. Isaac Sim is the underlying simulation and rendering platform, built on NVIDIA Omniverse and USD, using PhysX for physically accurate rigid-body and contact dynamics, with photorealistic ray-traced rendering: a general-purpose 3D simulation tool that happens to be very good for robotics. Isaac Lab is a robot-learning framework built on top of Isaac Sim: it wraps Isaac Sim scenes as standardised RL/imitation-learning environments (observation/action spaces, reward functions, episode reset logic) and, its single most practically important feature, runs thousands of instances of the same environment in parallel on one GPU. This is what turns “simulate a robot arm” into “simulate 4,096 robot arms simultaneously, all slightly different,” the throughput a modern RL training run actually needs. Isaac Sim is the right tool directly for a one-off scene or a non-learning simulation task; Isaac Lab is the right tool when the goal is training or evaluating a learned policy at scale.

62.2.2 Open-Source Alternatives

MuJoCo offers fast, accurate contact physics with basic (non-photorealistic) rendering and good CPU/GPU (MJX) parallelism, now free and open-source, extremely widely used as an RL benchmark. Gazebo offers solid, mature, ROS-native physics with a strong sensor-simulation ecosystem, suited to classical robotics/ROS workflows. PyBullet is lightweight and easy to prototype with, suited to fast prototyping and teaching. A learner without a GPU or Isaac licence should reach for MuJoCo or PyBullet to learn the concepts. This book leans Isaac-primary because it’s the tool most aligned with production robot-learning workflows, not because the alternatives are inadequate for learning.

62.2.3 Domain Randomization

The core mechanism for narrowing the sim-to-real gap: instead of training against one fixed simulator configuration, deliberately vary simulated parameters (physics: friction, mass, motor strength; visual: lighting, texture, camera intrinsics; sensor: noise characteristics) across a distribution during training. A policy trained against a single fixed simulator configuration can, and empirically does, learn to exploit that configuration’s specific, incorrect quirks, because doing so minimises training loss and the policy has no way to know those quirks aren’t real. A policy trained across many simulated configurations can’t do this. Overfitting to any one configuration actively hurts its performance on the others, so the only way to do well across the whole distribution is to learn features robust to the variation, and those tend to be closer to the features that also hold in reality.

62.2.4 Synthetic Data Generation

A related but distinct use of simulation: rendering many randomized scenes to generate labelled training data for perception models (object detection boxes, segmentation masks, depth maps) automatically and perfectly, because the simulator knows ground truth exactly (it placed every object itself). This sidesteps the expensive, error-prone, slow process of hand-labelling real photographs, at the cost of a visual domain gap between synthetic and real images that domain randomization, applied to rendering this time rather than physics, also helps close.

62.3 Trade-offs and Limits

A highly accurate simulator is slow, which limits how many training episodes are affordable; a fast, simplified one allows more training but risks a wider sim-to-real gap. Isaac Lab’s answer is parallelism: run many instances of a moderately accurate simulator simultaneously, rather than one instance of a maximally accurate one sequentially. Too narrow a randomization range and the sim-to-real gap isn’t meaningfully closed; too wide and the training problem becomes so varied the policy struggles to learn anything useful for any single configuration. Worked Example 62 measures a concrete instance of the narrow-range failure mode directly. Isaac Sim’s ray-traced rendering is far more visually realistic than MuJoCo’s, which matters for vision policies but costs real compute. A non-vision policy (joint-angle observations only) gets no benefit from paying that cost.

62.5 Worked Example

A PD controller’s gains tuned two ways for a robot arm carrying an unknown payload: fixed-config gains designed for one specific assumed mass (1.0kg, unloaded), versus domain-randomized gains designed against a geometric-mean payload mass (2.24kg) spanning a realistic 1–5kg range:

Both controllers evaluated on the identical test set: 300 payload masses sampled uniformly from 1.0 to 5.0 kg, a range the fixed-config controller was never designed to handle:

Controller Mean tracking cost Mean overshoot Max overshoot
Fixed-config (designed for 1.0kg) 0.6485 10.0% 20.5%
Domain-randomized (designed across 1–5kg) 0.4937 1.3% 5.5%

The domain-randomized controller wins decisively across the full test distribution: lower mean tracking cost, and dramatically lower overshoot (1.3% mean versus 10.0%, roughly 7.7x better). This alone confirms Exposition’s central claim, but the more striking result comes from checking the fixed-config controller’s own exact design point, the one condition it should have the advantage on:

Condition Fixed-config Domain-randomized
Unloaded (mass=1.0, fixed-config’s exact design target) cost=0.46205, overshoot=0.00% cost=0.41036, overshoot=0.00%
Heaviest realistic payload (mass=5.0) cost=0.83607, overshoot=20.54% cost=0.57765, overshoot=5.52%

Even at mass=1.0, the exact single value the fixed-config controller was tuned for, with no domain-randomization “handicap” for the comparison, the domain-randomized controller still achieves a lower tracking cost (0.41036 vs. 0.46205). This is a genuinely counterintuitive result worth sitting with: tuning for a range didn’t just avoid catastrophic failure elsewhere at the cost of peak performance at the design point; it won at the design point too. At the heaviest tested payload, the gap widens dramatically (cost 0.578 vs. 0.836, overshoot 5.52% vs. 20.54%), exactly where a fixed-config controller’s untested assumption breaks down hardest. This is Exposition’s mechanism made numeric: a policy (or controller) trained against a single fixed configuration optimises specifically for that configuration’s quirks in a way that doesn’t generalise, while one exposed to variation during training is forced to find a genuinely more robust solution, one that, in this case, wasn’t even a trade-off against peak single-point performance.

AWS SageMaker Notes

Isaac Lab training runs are commonly hosted on GPU-backed EC2 instances or SageMaker Training Jobs with ml.g5/ml.p4d instance types, since Isaac Lab’s core value, thousands of parallel environment instances on one GPU, needs the same GPU memory and compute budget regardless of where it runs. 62.9 Mental Model

Simulation is a cheap, safe, parallel substitute for the real world during training, and domain randomization is the acknowledgment that the substitute is never exact. Rather than trying to make the simulator perfectly accurate, which is impossible, the policy (or controller) is made indifferent to the ways the simulator is inaccurate, by never letting it see the same “reality” twice during training. This chapter’s Worked Example turned that principle into a number: a controller tuned across a range of payload masses beat one tuned for a single exact mass, even measured at that exact mass. Robustness, in this case, wasn’t a trade-off against peak performance, it was strictly better.
