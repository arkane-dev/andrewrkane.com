Chapter 62 gave the track a simulator to practice in. Chapter 61 gave it an architecture, a vision-language-action (VLA) model, that can produce an action given an observation and an instruction. Neither one, by itself, produces a good policy. Something has to actually train the weights: shape a randomly-initialised or pretrained network into one that reliably completes the task. This chapter covers the three practical ways to do that, and demonstrates, with real, measured numbers, the sharpest failure mode of the most sample-efficient one.

63.1 Motivation

A policy is a function from observation to action, exactly the same object Book 2, Chapter 35 (Reinforcement Learning & RLHF) called the “policy” when discussing RLHF. That’s not a coincidence; it’s the same underlying concept, reused. The difference is what the policy is being optimised to do: Book 2, Chapter 35’s policy is a language model being optimised to produce text a human would prefer; this chapter’s policy is a robot controller being optimised to complete a physical task. Same vocabulary, agent, environment, state, action, reward, policy, different environment, different reward, same math.

63.2 Exposition

63.2.1 Route 1: Reinforcement Learning

The policy is trained by trial and error against a reward signal, in exactly the RL framework Book 2, Chapter 35 introduced, except here the “environment” is a physics simulator (Chapter 62) and the reward is task-specific: distance reduced to a goal, a successful grasp detected, an episode completed without a collision. RL doesn’t need demonstrations. It discovers behaviour through exploration. But it typically needs a lot of trial-and-error episodes to converge, which is exactly why a simulator’s thousands-of-parallel-environments throughput (Chapter 62) exists.

Where RL struggles: reward design is hard. Get the reward wrong and the policy will optimise it faithfully, producing exactly the unintended behaviour the reward accidentally incentivised. A legged robot rewarded for forward velocity can learn to fall forward repeatedly rather than walk, if falling forward scores higher velocity than walking does.

63.2.2 Route 2: Imitation Learning / Behaviour Cloning

Instead of trial and error, collect (observation, expert-action) pairs, from a human teleoperating the robot, or a scripted expert policy, and train the policy via ordinary supervised learning to imitate them. This is dramatically more sample-efficient than RL when demonstrations are available: no exploration needed, no reward function needed, just regression.

Where imitation learning struggles, and this is the whole point of this chapter’s Worked Example: a behaviour-cloned policy is only as good as the states its demonstrations covered. Real deployment inevitably visits states slightly off the demonstrated trajectories, small errors accumulate, or the robot starts in an unusual position, and once it’s even a little off-distribution, the policy has no idea what to do, because it was never trained as a general function of state, only as a fit to the specific states demonstrated.

This is the classic distribution shift problem in imitation learning, and its standard mitigation is DAgger (Dataset Aggregation): instead of collecting all demonstrations up front from the expert’s own trajectories, iteratively roll out the trained policy, find the states it visits (which drift away from the original demonstration distribution as training proceeds), query the expert for the correct action at those states, add those corrections to the training set, and retrain. This directly targets the actual problem, the policy’s own drift, rather than hoping more demonstrations from the original distribution will somehow cover states the policy will visit but the expert never did.

dataset ← initial expert demonstrations
policy ← train_behaviour_cloning(dataset)

repeat for N iterations:
    # NOT expert rollouts
    visited_states ← rollout(policy, various starts)
    corrections ← [
        (s, expert_action(s)) for s in visited_states
    ]
    dataset ← dataset ∪ corrections
    # retrain on aggregated set
    policy ← train_behaviour_cloning(dataset)

DAgger requires an expert that can be queried at arbitrary states (a scripted controller, a human in the loop, or an optimal planner) not just a fixed set of pre-recorded trajectories. Running one DAgger iteration and assuming the problem is solved is a common mistake: distribution shift shrinks with iterations, it doesn’t disappear after one.

63.2.3 Route 3: Fine-Tuning a Pretrained VLA Policy

Instead of training from scratch, via RL or imitation learning, start from a VLA model’s (Chapter 61) broad, internet-pretrained visual-language-action competence, and fine-tune it with a comparatively small amount of task-specific data, using the same parameter-efficient fine-tuning machinery (LoRA) as Book 2, Chapter 34’s instruction fine-tuning, just applied to a VLA checkpoint instead of a language model.

Why this is often the best starting point in practice: the pretrained VLA already has broad visual and instruction-following competence; fine-tuning only needs to teach it the specific task, which requires far less task-specific data than training a policy from nothing. The catch: it inherits whatever biases and blind spots the pretrained VLA has, and, being built on a much larger model than a from-scratch policy, is typically far more expensive to run at inference time, which is exactly why Chapter 64 (Edge Compute & IoT Networking) exists.

63.2.4 Choosing Between the Three

Question Favours
Do you have a clear, well-shaped reward function and time for lots of simulated trial and error? RL
Do you have (or can you collect) good expert demonstrations covering a wide range of states, including recovery from mistakes? Imitation learning
Do you need broad generalisation to novel objects/instructions and can afford a large model at inference time? VLA fine-tuning
Is inference latency extremely tight (recall Chapter 58’s control-loop frequency constraints)? RL or imitation learning with a small, fast network, not a multi-billion-parameter VLA

In practice, production systems often combine routes: fine-tune a VLA for broad task competence, then apply a lightweight RL fine-tuning pass, close to what RLHF-style methods do for language models in Book 2, Chapter 35, applied here to actions, to sharpen performance on the specific deployment task.

63.3 Trade-offs and Limits

Sample efficiency vs. generality. Imitation learning is the most sample-efficient when good demonstrations exist, but generalises only as well as the demonstration coverage, which this chapter’s Worked Example shows can fail catastrophically, not gracefully. RL generalises across whatever the training distribution (including domain randomization, Chapter 62) covered, at the cost of needing far more training episodes.

Reward design vs. demonstration collection. RL trades the cost of designing a correct reward function for the cost of collecting demonstrations. Neither is free; which is cheaper depends entirely on the task.

Confident failure is worse than obvious failure. The most important lesson from this chapter’s Worked Example isn’t the specific numbers. It’s that a cloned policy fails confidently: it doesn’t hesitate, flag uncertainty, or refuse to act near an obstacle it’s never seen. It just does the wrong thing as smoothly as it does the right thing in-distribution. Any physical AI system built on imitation learning needs either DAgger-style iterative correction, an explicit out-of-distribution detector, or a safety layer that doesn’t trust the policy blindly outside its known-good region, exactly what Chapter 65 (Deployment, Fleets & Safety) covers.

63.4 Procedures

Procedure 63.1 (Design a Reward Function Without Shooting Yourself in the Foot). When to use: setting up an RL training run for a robot control task. Reward shaping pitfalls, in order of how often they bite:

  1. Too sparse. Reward only on task completion (e.g. +1 only when the goal is reached) gives the policy almost no signal to learn from during early training, when it rarely succeeds by chance. Fix: add a dense shaping term (e.g. negative distance-to-goal each step) alongside the sparse completion bonus.
  2. Reward hacking. An overly specific dense reward can be “solved” in an unintended way that technically maximises reward without doing the task.
  3. Conflicting terms. Summing many reward terms with hand-tuned weights (task progress + smoothness + energy penalty + safety margin…) can produce a policy that satisfies none of them well, because gradients from competing terms partially cancel. Start with the minimum viable reward, usually one or two terms, verify it produces sane behaviour, and add terms incrementally.
  4. Scale mismatches. If one term ranges in [0,1] and another in [0,1000], the larger-scale term dominates learning regardless of intended weighting. Normalise reward term scales before combining.

Common mistake: debugging a policy that’s “not learning” by tweaking the network architecture or learning rate, when the actual problem is the reward function itself. Always sanity-check the reward on a few known-good and known-bad trajectories, does it score them the way you’d expect, before spending compute on a full training run.

Procedure 63.2 (Build a Scripted Expert and Roll Out Demonstrations). When to use: generating an imitation-learning dataset when a closed-form controller for the task exists or can be written.

Procedure 63.3 (Train a Behaviour-Cloning Policy by Supervised Regression). When to use: fitting a policy directly to (state, expert-action) pairs, once a demonstration set exists.

Procedure 63.4 (Test a Policy Both In-Distribution and Out-of-Distribution). When to use: evaluating any imitation-learned policy before trusting it. Never evaluate only on states that resemble training data. Roll out the trained policy from a batch of starting states matching the demonstration distribution, then from a second batch deliberately chosen to lie outside it (e.g. beside an obstacle the demonstrations never approached), and compare success and failure rates directly. Common mistake: reporting only the in-distribution result. It looks like a finished, working policy and hides the failure mode entirely.

Procedure 63.5 (Fine-Tune a VLA Policy with LoRA). When to use: a pretrained VLA checkpoint (Chapter 61) exists and a modest amount of task-specific demonstration data is available. This directly reuses Book 2, Chapter 34’s LoRA/PEFT approach, applied to a VLA checkpoint instead of a text-only language model. Its rank/alpha/target-module selection guidance applies here unchanged.

63.5 Worked Example

The chapter’s tutorial builds a point-mass agent navigating a 2D plane to a fixed target at (8, 8), starting from rest near the origin. A circular obstacle sits at (1.5, 7.0), close enough to matter if the agent ever passes near it, far enough that a direct path from near the origin to the target never comes close to it. The scripted expert (Listing 63.1) is a PD controller with a repulsive term that activates only within a 1.2-unit trigger radius of the obstacle: a smooth, well-defined function of state that works correctly everywhere, including states it’s never been evaluated at before, because it’s a formula, not something fit to data.

Eighty demonstrations were collected, every one starting at rest somewhere in the small region (0-2, 0-2) near the origin, a deliberate limitation. The straight-line path from there to the target never comes near the obstacle, so the demonstrations never show the policy how to avoid it:

This collected 4,400 (state, action) pairs from 80 demonstrations; the closest any training trajectory ever got to the obstacle was 2.61 units, well outside the 1.2-unit trigger radius, confirmed by the printed False. A behaviour-cloning policy (Listing 63.2, a 4-32-32-2 MLP) trained on these pairs for 400 epochs, MSE loss falling from 4.286 at epoch 0 to 0.015 at epoch 399.

Tested in-distribution, starting from the same near-origin region as training (Listing 63.2’s setup, evaluated with Procedure 63.4), the cloned policy reached the target 40/40 times with zero collisions. It looks like a perfectly good policy.

Tested out-of-distribution, starting from a ring of positions right beside the obstacle, close enough that avoidance is needed but not so close the agent starts already colliding, the same policy reached the target 0/40 times. Not “worse.” Zero. The scripted expert, evaluated at the exact same starting states, reached the target 40/40 times, because its policy is a general formula, not something fit only to the states it happened to be demonstrated at:

Output: the cloned policy reached the target 0/40 times, hitting the obstacle on 11/40 trials; the expert reached the target 40/40 times, with 6/40 trials passing close enough to register as a near-collision along its (successful) avoidance manoeuvre. This is the distribution-shift problem, made concrete: a behaviour-cloned policy has no idea what to do outside the distribution of states its demonstrations covered, and it doesn’t know that it doesn’t know. It doesn’t fail gracefully or cautiously. It confidently outputs something, because a neural network always outputs something, and that something has no reason to be sensible outside the training distribution. The fix isn’t more demonstrations from the same region, Procedure 63.1’s sibling lesson for imitation learning, it’s DAgger: demonstrations from the states the trained policy itself tends to visit and get wrong.

63.10 Mental Model

RL learns from consequences it experiences directly. Imitation learning learns from consequences someone else already experienced and wrote down. VLA fine-tuning starts from a model that’s already seen an enormous amount of someone else’s visual and linguistic experience, and only needs a little of your own task’s consequences to specialise. All three eventually answer the same question, “what should I do right now?”, from different amounts and kinds of prior information. And imitation learning’s genuinely useful sample efficiency comes with a genuinely sharp edge: a policy fit only to the states it was shown has nothing to say about the states it wasn’t.
