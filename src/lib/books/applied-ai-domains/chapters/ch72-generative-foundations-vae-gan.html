Every model in this book’s image chapters so far takes a picture in and produces a judgement about it: a class label, a bounding box, a segmentation mask. None of them produce a new picture. This chapter starts a different lineage, models that generate, and it does so by building the same small piece of machinery three different ways: a plain autoencoder, a Variational Autoencoder (VAE), and a Generative Adversarial Network (GAN). The point is not that one of these is simply better than the others. It’s that each represents a genuinely different bet about what makes generation possible, and understanding why the field moved from the first to the second to the third, in that order, is what makes diffusion models, the next chapter, look like an answer to specific, nameable problems rather than an arbitrary new idea.

72.1 Motivation

Reconstruction is not generation. A network can compress an image down to a small vector and reconstruct it almost perfectly, without that vector being usable for creating new, plausible images that were never in the training set. This distinction sounds abstract until it’s measured directly: an autoencoder trained only to reconstruct its inputs learns some latent representation, but nothing in its training ever constrained what the overall shape of that representation looks like. Sample a fresh point from a guessed distribution, the obvious next move, and unless you got lucky, the decoder receives an input from a region of latent space it never learned to make sense of. The rest of this chapter is the story of two structurally different fixes for that gap: regularise the latent space directly (the VAE), or throw the whole reconstruction framing away and train by adversarial competition instead (the GAN).

72.2 Exposition

72.2.1 The Autoencoder’s Structural Gap

An autoencoder is an encoder, a bottleneck, and a decoder, trained end-to-end to minimize reconstruction error, typically mean squared error between the input and the reconstruction. Nothing about that objective says anything about the shape of the bottleneck representation across the training set. It could be wildly scaled, oddly shaped, or full of gaps where no real image maps to anything nearby. That’s fine for the task the autoencoder was actually trained for (compression and reconstruction) and useless for the task it wasn’t: sampling a brand-new point and getting something recognisable back out.

72.2.2 The VAE’s Fix: Regularize the Latent Space Itself

A Variational Autoencoder changes the objective, not just the architecture. Instead of the encoder outputting a single point, it outputs the parameters of a distribution, a mean and a log-variance, and a sample is drawn from that distribution via the reparameterisation trick: z=μ+ε⋅σz = \mu + \varepsilon \cdot \sigmaz=μ+ε⋅σ, with ε∼N(0,I)\varepsilon \sim N(0, I)ε∼N(0,I) sampled independently of the network’s parameters. Because the randomness is isolated in ε\varepsilonε, zzz remains a differentiable function of μ\muμ and σ\sigmaσ, and the whole thing trains end-to-end with ordinary backpropagation. Sampling directly from Normal(mu, std) would not be differentiable with respect to mu and std, which is precisely the problem the trick sidesteps.

The loss adds a second term to plain reconstruction loss: a KL-divergence term pulling the aggregate distribution of encoded points toward a standard normal, N(0,I)N(0, I)N(0,I). For a diagonal Gaussian encoder, this term has a closed form:

The weight β\betaβ controls where the model sits on a genuine trade-off, not a strict improvement: β=1.0\beta = 1.0β=1.0 is the standard VAE; β<1\beta < 1β<1 prioritizes reconstruction fidelity over latent-space structure; β>1\beta > 1β>1 (a beta-VAE) trades more reconstruction quality for a more strongly regularized, often more interpretable, latent space. Push β\betaβ too high and the model can fall into posterior collapse: KL loss goes to nearly zero because the decoder has learned to ignore the latent code entirely, producing generic, blurry reconstructions regardless of what zzz it’s given.

72.2.3 GANs: A Structurally Different Answer

A Generative Adversarial Network doesn’t use an encoder or a reconstruction loss at all. A generator network maps random noise directly to images; a discriminator network is trained simultaneously to distinguish generated images from real ones; the generator is trained to fool an improving discriminator. Neither network is ever told directly what a good image looks like. The discriminator’s judgement, itself constantly improving, is the only signal the generator ever receives.

This structural difference explains a real, observable effect: GAN samples tend to be sharper than VAE samples. A VAE’s pixel-wise reconstruction loss (MSE, say) is minimized, under genuine ambiguity about what the “right” reconstruction is, by averaging over plausible options, which produces blur. A GAN’s discriminator doesn’t average anything; it just judges “does this look real,” a judgement that doesn’t reward blur the way pixel-wise MSE does.

The cost is that GAN training is a two-network adversarial game, not a single loss being minimized, and that game can fail in ways a straightforward optimization cannot. Mode collapse is the generator finding a small handful of outputs that reliably fool the discriminator and simply stopping exploration beyond them. General training instability is the two networks failing to reach any stable equilibrium at all, oscillating rather than converging. Both failure modes are, notoriously, invisible in the loss curves themselves: a GAN’s loss doesn’t behave like a supervised model’s loss, so trusting it the way one would trust a falling cross-entropy curve is a mistake. Diagnosing GAN training health requires checking discriminator balance and sample diversity directly, which is exactly what this chapter’s Worked Example does.

72.3 Trade-offs and Limits

AE vs. VAE. If the task is only compression and reconstruction, a plain autoencoder is simpler and reconstructs better. There’s no reason to pay the VAE’s regularization tax. If the task requires generating new, plausible samples, the AE’s unconstrained latent space makes that essentially a gamble, and the VAE’s KL term is the price of admission for a latent space that is actually sample-able.

VAE vs. GAN. The VAE’s likelihood-based training is comparatively stable, gives an interpretable, structured latent space “for free,” and has a well-behaved single loss to monitor, at the cost of blurrier outputs. The GAN’s adversarial training can produce sharper outputs but is harder to stabilize, has no principled likelihood to fall back on for diagnosing training health, and requires active monitoring of things like discriminator balance and sample diversity rather than a single number trending toward zero.

Neither is a strict upgrade of the other, and neither is the field’s final answer. Chapter 73 picks up exactly where this leaves off, with diffusion models built as a direct response to both VAE blurriness and GAN instability.

72.4 Procedures

Procedure 72.1 (Train a Plain Autoencoder and Probe Its Latent Space). When to use: as a baseline before building anything more elaborate. Establishing that reconstruction quality and generative usability are different things.

Common mistake: after training, sampling a “new” image by drawing z ~ N(0, 1) and decoding it, then treating a noisy result as a bug. It isn’t. It’s the AE’s latent space simply never having been asked to match N(0,1)N(0,1)N(0,1). Check the latent space’s own statistics (z_train.mean(), z_train.std()) before assuming a sampling procedure should work.

Procedure 72.2 (Implement the Reparameterisation Trick and the VAE Loss). When to use: building any VAE. This is the mechanism that makes it trainable end-to-end.

Rule of thumb: if generated samples look like noise while reconstruction is excellent, BETA is too low. The latent space isn’t regularized enough to be sample-able. If reconstructions turn blurry and generic and the KL loss sits near zero, BETA is too high: posterior collapse.

Procedure 72.3 (Diagnose Discriminator/Generator Balance). When to use: after any GAN training run, before trusting the loss curves or the samples.

Discriminator accuracy (real / fake) Meaning Action
~0.5 / ~0.5 Balanced equilibrium, healthy training Nothing: this is what you want
~1.0 / ~1.0 D has “won”, G’s gradient signal is vanishing Slow D down: fewer D updates, smaller LR, label smoothing
~0.5 / ~1.0 (D calls everything fake) Likely a sign/scaling bug in generator output Verify generator output range matches D’s expected input range
Wildly oscillating, never settling Learning rates too high, no stabilization applied Apply the stabilization recipe in Procedure 72.4

Procedure 72.4 (Check for Mode Collapse and Stabilize GAN Training). When to use: discriminator balance looks reasonable but samples look repetitive, or training oscillates without settling.

Standard stabilization tricks, roughly in the order worth trying first: label smoothing (use 0.9 instead of 1.0 for “real” labels in the discriminator’s loss, so it doesn’t become overconfident and starve the generator’s gradient); balancing update frequency (if D consistently wins, give it fewer updates per generator update, or a smaller learning rate); LeakyReLU rather than plain ReLU in the discriminator, to avoid dead neurons that stop passing gradient back to the generator; Adam with betas=(0.5, 0.999) rather than the default (0.9, 0.999), which reduces momentum-driven oscillation and is close to universal in GAN training; and, if none of that resolves it, a different loss formulation entirely (e.g. Wasserstein GAN with gradient penalty), which addresses instability at a more fundamental level than the tricks above.

72.5 Worked Example

The tutorial notebook behind this chapter trains all three architectures, AE, VAE, GAN, on the same 8x8 grayscale digits dataset (sklearn.datasets.load_digits, 1,437 training / 360 test images), so the comparison between them is apples-to-apples rather than across different data.

The plain autoencoder (Procedure 72.1, latent dimension 8) reached a test-set reconstruction MSE of 0.0200, and its latent space came out with mean −0.845-0.845−0.845, standard deviation 4.019, and a range of roughly [−11.81,12.25][-11.81, 12.25][−11.81,12.25], nowhere close to the N(0,1)N(0,1)N(0,1) distribution an “obvious” sampling attempt would draw from. Feeding the decoder 16 vectors sampled from an actual N(0,1)N(0,1)N(0,1) produced outputs with mean 0.297 and standard deviation 0.181, visually, not recognizable digits, because the decoder was never asked to make sense of inputs in that region of latent space.

Sampling the same 16 N(0,1)N(0,1)N(0,1) vectors used against the AE and decoding them through the VAE produced outputs that were recognizably digit-shaped, because this time the sampling distribution matched what the decoder was actually trained against.

72.6 History and Further Reading

Kingma and Welling’s “Auto-Encoding Variational Bayes” (Kingma and Welling 2013) introduced the VAE and the reparameterisation trick this chapter builds directly. Goodfellow et al.’s “Generative Adversarial Networks” (Goodfellow et al. 2014) introduced the adversarial training framework, including the mode collapse and instability failure modes this chapter’s diagnostics target. Salimans et al.’s “Improved Techniques for Training GANs” (Salimans et al. 2016) is the source of several of the stabilization tricks in Procedure 72.4, including label smoothing.

72.7 How This Connects

This chapter builds on this book’s coverage of convolutional and residual architectures. The encoder, decoder, generator, and discriminator networks here are all built from the same conv-layer vocabulary those chapters introduced. It also draws on Book 2, Chapter 28 (Intro to Neural Networks) for the backpropagation and optimization mechanics that make training any of these three architectures possible at all. It enables Chapter 73 (Diffusion Models) directly: diffusion is best understood as a response to specific, named limitations of both VAEs (blurry samples) and GANs (unstable training, mode collapse), which is exactly why this chapter exists first. It is often confused with treating “autoencoder” and “generative model” as interchangeable. A plain autoencoder, as this chapter’s Worked Example showed directly, is not a generative model on its own; nothing about its training makes its latent space sample-able.

72.9 Mental Model

An autoencoder answers “can this be compressed and reconstructed?” A VAE answers “can this be compressed into a space I can also sample new points from?” A GAN answers “can I train a generator by having it fool an ever-improving judge, with no explicit reconstruction target at all?” Each is a genuinely different bet about what makes generation possible, not a strict upgrade of the last, and the trade-offs each one makes are exactly what the next chapter’s diffusion models were built to route around.
