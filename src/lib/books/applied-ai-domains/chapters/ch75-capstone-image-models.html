Every chapter in this track taught one piece in isolation, on a task built to make that piece’s lesson land cleanly. Chapter 69’s transfer-learning exercises used a backbone that already existed. Chapter 72’s VAE generated unconditionally, with no downstream use for what it produced. Chapter 74’s evaluation metrics were computed with a purpose-built toy CLIP, not a model built for anything else. Each isolation was the right pedagogical choice at the time, but a real project rarely gets to keep its perception, generation, and evaluation pieces neatly separated, and the interactions between pieces are exactly where a track built chapter-by-chapter tends to reveal something a single-topic chapter can’t. This capstone forces one of those interactions: train a real backbone for a real classification task, then reuse that exact backbone, not a fresh, unrelated network, as both the conditioning signal for a generator and the feature extractor for evaluating what the generator produces.

75.1 Motivation

The capstone chains three pieces into one pipeline on a single small dataset (sklearn’s 8x8 digits). A small ResNet-style backbone (Chapter 69) is pretrained on 8 digit classes only, then adapted to 2 entirely unseen classes with just 8 labelled examples per class, comparing feature extraction against fine-tuning, the same decision Chapter 69 built a reference table for. A class-conditional VAE (Chapter 72) generates new digit images, conditioned not on a raw one-hot label but on the backbone’s own learned embedding for each class: an explicit dependency between the discriminative and generative halves, not two unrelated demos bolted together. The generated images are then scored using FID-style and class-conditional-accuracy metrics computed with the same backbone, reused as the feature extractor (Chapter 74), exactly the choice a team without ImageNet-scale infrastructure has to make in practice.

75.2 Exposition

75.2.1 Chaining Real Pieces Together Surfaces What Isolated Demos Cannot

Building three separate demos, one per chapter, cannot surface a bug that only exists at the seam between two of them. This capstone’s pipeline has exactly two such seams: the backbone feeding the generator’s conditioning signal, and the backbone feeding the evaluation metrics. Both produced genuine findings that no single-chapter tutorial would have caught: a real evaluation bug, and a real hyperparameter failure mode, described in full below.

75.2.2 The Conditioning Signal Is the Backbone’s Own Embedding, Not a Fresh Label

Rather than conditioning the generator on a one-hot class label the way Chapter 73’s diffusion model did, this capstone conditions on the backbone’s embed() output: a per-class mean taken over the full dataset. This carries a real consequence: the conditioning vector inherits whatever the backbone actually learned to separate classes by, including cases where separation is weak. The backbone was never trained on classes 8 and 9, and their class-embedding distance measured noticeably closer (1.35) than most pretrained-class pairs (2.6-7.6). The conditioning signal for those two classes genuinely carries less separating information, a direct, measured consequence of the backbone never having seen them during pretraining, not a modeling choice made for convenience.

75.2.3 A Frozen Backbone Must Actually Be Frozen

BatchNorm layers behave differently in training mode (normalizing using the statistics of the current batch) versus evaluation mode (normalizing using running statistics accumulated during training). A backbone that is only ever read from after training, as a frozen feature extractor, a conditioning source, or an evaluation metric, needs .eval() called on it explicitly, exactly the same discipline torch.no_grad() gets for gradients. Skipping this is not a cosmetic mistake: a small batch of generated images that are unusually similar to each other can produce per-batch normalization statistics that amplify noise instead of removing it, corrupting every embedding the “frozen” backbone produces from that point forward.

75.2.4 Classification Correctness and Distributional Realism Are Different Things

A downstream classifier recognizing a generated sample as its intended class is necessary evidence a generator works, but it is not sufficient evidence the generator is good. A model can satisfy a classification metric completely while still being measurably less diverse and less distributionally realistic than real data. This is exactly the gap Chapter 74 built FID, Inception Score, and CLIPScore to catch, and exactly the reason a generative model should never be certified as “working” on classifier accuracy alone.

75.3 Trade-offs and Limits

Fine-tuning beat feature extraction on the few-shot transfer task, 95.6% versus 87.9% accuracy. With only 16 total labelled examples across both held-out classes, unfreezing the residual block and training it at a small learning rate still generalized better than keeping the backbone frozen. This is consistent with Chapter 69’s guidance that fine-tuning tends to win once there is some labelled data, even a little, but it is not free: fine-tuning risks catastrophic forgetting of the pretrained classes if the learning rate or epoch count is too aggressive, which is why the backbone’s learning rate (1e-4) was kept an order of magnitude below the new head’s (1e-2).

The generator produces recognizable but not fully realistic digits. Every single generated sample was classified as its intended class: the conditioning genuinely works. But FID against real data measured roughly 32x higher than the real-vs-real sanity baseline, and sample diversity measured far below real data’s own diversity. This is the same VAE blurriness and mode-narrowing Chapter 72 documented directly: the model learned “what makes a 3 look like a 3” well enough to fool a classifier, without learning the full spread of ways a 3 can legitimately look. A production version of this same pipeline would need a real-resolution backbone rather than a 5,000-parameter toy, a pretrained diffusion model rather than a from-scratch VAE for generation quality, a real Inception-v3 or domain-specific feature extractor for FID rather than reusing a small task-specific backbone, and a labelled evaluation set large enough that FID’s covariance estimate is not as noisy as it is at toy scale. The shape of the pipeline (backbone, adapt it, generate from it, evaluate honestly) carries over unchanged; only the scale of each piece needs to grow.

75.4 Procedures

Procedure 75.1 (Reuse a Trained Backbone as a Generator’s Conditioning Signal). When to use: a trained classifier exists, and a generator is wanted whose outputs are tied to that classifier’s learned representation of each class, rather than an arbitrary one-hot label.

Why this matters: the conditioning vector now carries whatever the backbone actually learned to separate classes by, including it being poorly separated for classes the backbone was never trained on. This capstone’s held-out classes 8/9 embeddings measured a distance of 1.35 apart, versus 2.6-7.6 for most pretrained-class pairs.

Procedure 75.2 (Avoid the BatchNorm Eval-Mode Bug). When to use: any time a model containing BatchNorm layers is trained and then reused read-only afterward, as a frozen feature extractor, a conditioning source, or an evaluation metric.

Symptom if this is skipped: predictions or embeddings on data that differs from the training distribution, such as small batches of generated images that are unusually similar to each other, can come back essentially random, because BatchNorm in training mode normalizes using statistics computed from that batch alone. Rule of thumb: call .eval() immediately after the last training loop that updates a model’s parameters, before it is used for anything else in the rest of the pipeline. This is the same discipline as wrapping inference in torch.no_grad().

Procedure 75.3 (Diagnose and Fix Posterior Collapse in a Conditional VAE). When to use: a VAE’s KL loss converges to approximately zero and generated samples don’t vary with the latent code or the conditioning signal.

Why this matters: this is the same failure mode Chapter 72 named directly (“reconstructions are blurry or generic, KL loss near zero” means beta is too high), reappearing whenever a new VAE is built, regardless of what it is conditioned on. Always check the KL loss term separately from the total loss. A total loss that looks fine can hide a KL term that has quietly gone to zero.

Procedure 75.4 (Choose Between Classifier Accuracy and Distributional Metrics When Evaluating a Generator). When to use: deciding whether a generative model is good enough to ship.

Why this matters: this chapter’s generator scored a perfect 1.0000 on class-conditional accuracy while still being measurably less diverse and less distributionally realistic than real data. A model can satisfy one metric completely and still fail another. Ship decisions should check both.

75.5 Worked Example

The tutorial notebook behind this chapter runs the full pipeline end to end: a backbone pretrained on 8 digit classes, few-shot transfer to 2 held-out classes, a class-conditional VAE generator built on the backbone’s own embeddings, and honest evaluation of the result. Every number below is measured from running the notebook, including the ones that are less than perfect.

Two genuine bugs surfaced during development of this pipeline, neither of which a single-chapter tutorial would have caught. First, an early version of this notebook never called backbone.eval() before reusing it as a frozen feature extractor (Procedure 75.2). The result: generated-sample class-conditional accuracy came back at exactly chance (8 out of 80, 10%), which looked like a broken generator. It wasn’t. BatchNorm was computing normalization statistics from each small batch of generated images instead of using the running statistics learned during training, because the backbone was still nominally in training mode. A backbone that scores 96.9% via a linear probe on real held-out data can still produce garbage embeddings on a different kind of input if one line is missing.

Second, and independently, the VAE was first trained with BETA=0.5, Chapter 72’s default, and suffered posterior collapse: the KL loss collapsed to approximately 0, meaning the decoder learned to ignore both the latent code and the class conditioning entirely (Procedure 75.3). This is the exact failure mode Chapter 72 itself named and warned about, rediscovered independently here rather than looked up: a small, genuine confirmation that a prior chapter’s documented failure modes are real, not just textbook warnings. Lowering BETA to 0.1 fixed it, landing at a healthily non-zero final KL loss of 0.0197.

With both fixed, the measured result is a real success, but not a perfect one, exactly as Procedure 75.4 predicts it should be checked. Every single generated sample was classified as its intended class (1.0000): the conditioning genuinely works. But FID against real data (2.7411) measured roughly 32x higher than the real-vs-real sanity baseline (0.0856), and sample diversity (0.442) measured far below real data’s own diversity (1.920). Classification correctness and distributional realism are different things, measured by different metrics, and this pipeline’s own numbers show a real, measured gap between them.

Book 2, Chapter 37 (Capstone Project)’s scoping discipline (every stage gets a real, if modest, version, rather than doing one stage impressively and skipping the rest) applies directly here, and this chapter’s pipeline follows it: backbone, transfer learning, generation, and evaluation are all present and all genuinely measured, including the parts that came back less than perfect. 75.7 How This Connects

This chapter builds on every earlier chapter in this track, and specifically reuses three pieces outright: Chapter 69 (ResNet & Deep Architectures)‘s residual block and feature-extraction-versus-fine-tuning framework, Chapter 72 (Generative Foundations)’s VAE architecture and reparameterization trick, and Chapter 74 (Multimodal Models & Evaluation)’s FID formula and diversity check. This is the final chapter of the image models track within this book, and it enables nothing further here. It is often confused with a simple “put it all together” demo that just chains previous chapters’ code with no new insight. The point of a capstone is not the chaining. It is finding out what breaks, or degrades, or needs re-measuring, once the pieces are no longer tested in isolation. This chapter found a real evaluation bug (BatchNorm in train mode) and a real hyperparameter failure mode (posterior collapse) that neither Chapter 69, 72, nor 74 individually would have surfaced.

75.9 Mental Model

Every chapter in this track taught a tool. This capstone is the reminder that tools interact, and that some of the most useful things a capstone teaches aren’t in any single chapter’s syllabus: forgetting to call .eval() on a backbone being reused, or a hyperparameter that looked fine in isolation collapsing once it’s wired into a specific model, are the kind of bugs that only show up when pieces are actually connected, not documented in a vacuum.
