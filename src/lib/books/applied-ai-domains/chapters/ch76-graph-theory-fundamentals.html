Most of the data this book has covered so far is either tabular (rows and columns) or sequential (tokens in a sentence). Neither shape captures a fact like “vendor A shares a parent company with vendor B, who was flagged for a compliance issue two hops away.” That’s not a row you can filter or a sequence you can scan. It’s a relationship between entities, and relationships between entities are exactly what a graph represents natively. This chapter is the mathematical foundation the rest of this track builds on: every later chapter (graph databases, knowledge graph construction, graph neural networks, and beyond) is, underneath, doing traversal, path-finding, centrality, or community detection on some graph. Understanding those four operations here means every later chapter is “the same four ideas, applied to a new kind of graph,” not four separate new topics.

76.1 Motivation

A graph is just nodes (entities) and edges (relationships between them): deliberately the simplest possible data structure for representing connections. Everything this chapter covers is a different question you can ask of that same structure: what can I reach, and in what order do I discover it? (traversal); what’s the cheapest way to get from A to B? (shortest path); which nodes matter, and in what sense? (centrality); which nodes cluster together more tightly than they connect to everything else? (community detection). None of these require anything beyond the raw structure: no labels, no machine learning, no training data. That’s what makes graph theory the right starting point: it’s a set of exact, deterministic algorithms operating directly on structure, before this track introduces anything probabilistic or learned, which starts in Chapter 79 (Graph Neural Networks).

76.2 Exposition

76.2.1 BFS vs. DFS: Two Different Discovery Orders, Not Two Ways to the Same Answer

Breadth-first search explores in “layers”: everything one hop away, then everything two hops away, and so on, using a queue (first-in-first-out). This is why BFS finds a shortest (fewest-hops) path: by the time it reaches a node for the first time, it has necessarily already exhausted every shorter route to it. Depth-first search instead commits to a path and follows it as far as it goes before backtracking, using a stack (last-in-first-out, whether explicit or via recursion). Neither is “better”: they answer different questions. BFS is what you want for “what’s the minimum hops to X”; DFS is what you want for “trace this one dependency chain exhaustively.” Both are O(V+E)O(V+E)O(V+E), the same asymptotic cost, so the choice between them is about the question being asked, never about performance.

76.2.2 Shortest Path: Hops Are Not Cost

nx.shortest_path (unweighted BFS underneath) treats every edge as identically costly: one hop. nx.dijkstra_path instead minimises the sum of edge weights along a route, which is what you actually want when edges have real, unequal costs (latency, distance, risk). Dijkstra’s algorithm works by always expanding the currently cheapest known frontier node next, a greedy strategy that happens to be provably correct here, given non-negative edge weights, which is also why it fails silently on negative edge weights (a different algorithm, Bellman-Ford, is needed there). If the fewest-hops path and the lowest-cost path happen to agree on a particular graph, that’s a coincidence of that graph, not a general rule; on a graph with more varied weights they can easily diverge.

76.2.3 Centrality: Three Measures, Three Different Definitions of “Important”

  • Degree centrality just counts direct connections: how many neighbours a node has, normalised by graph size. Cheap, local, but blind to a node’s position in the rest of the graph.
  • Betweenness centrality counts what fraction of all shortest paths between other node pairs pass through this node. High betweenness means a lot of the graph’s traffic has no way around you.
  • PageRank (originally designed for ranking web pages by incoming-link importance) treats being linked-to by other important nodes as a recursive definition of your own importance.

These are genuinely different claims, not three views of the same fact. “Everything depends on me” (high PageRank, being a heavily-referenced sink) and “a lot of traffic has no way around me” (high betweenness, being a structural bottleneck on many shortest paths) can point at completely different nodes in the same graph. This chapter’s Worked Example measures exactly that split. A customer asking “what’s our single point of failure?” deserves to hear both answers, because losing either node breaks the system for a different structural reason.

76.2.4 Community Detection: Structure Alone, Checked Against Ground Truth

Louvain community detection maximises modularity, roughly, “how much more densely connected are nodes within these groups than you’d expect if edges were placed at random, given each node’s degree?”, using only the graph’s edges, with no external labels. Community detection finds structural clusters; those usually, not always, line up with whatever “real” grouping you suspect exists. The only way to know whether the structure found means anything real is to check it against ground truth when ground truth is available. This chapter’s Worked Example does exactly that, on a real, historically-documented social network.

76.3 Trade-offs and Limits

BFS/DFS choice is about the question, not performance. Both are O(V+E)O(V+E)O(V+E); pick based on what you need to know (shallow discovery order vs. exhaustive single-path tracing), not speed.

Unweighted vs. weighted shortest path. Using plain BFS-based shortest path when edges actually have unequal real-world costs (latency, risk, distance, price) gives an answer to a question you probably didn’t mean to ask. Reach for dijkstra_path whenever a weight attribute genuinely means something.

Centrality measure choice changes the answer, not just the framing. Picking degree centrality alone can completely miss a node’s role as a structural bottleneck if its degree happens to tie with another node’s while its betweenness is dramatically higher. There is no single “importance” score; there are three different, equally legitimate questions.

Community detection is unsupervised. It has no way to know the “right” number of communities or the “correct” grouping; it finds whatever structure the modularity objective rewards. Without ground truth, the modularity score itself (roughly, values above 0.3 suggest real structure; values near zero suggest the “communities” found aren’t meaningfully denser than random) is the only sanity check available.

76.4 Procedures

Procedure 76.1 (Build a Graph from Entities and Relationships). When to use: the first step for any new dataset of entities and their connections: services, people, documents, anything.

Decision: use DiGraph when the relationship is one-directional and that direction matters (calls, follows, cites). Use Graph when it’s inherently symmetric (friendship, co-occurrence, shared attribute).

Procedure 76.2 (Choose and Run a Traversal or Shortest-Path Algorithm). When to use: answering “what’s reachable,” “trace this path,” or “what’s the cheapest route.”

Warning: nx.dijkstra_path requires non-negative edge weights. If weights can be negative (a “cost” that’s sometimes a rebate), use nx.bellman_ford_path instead: slower, but correct in that case.

Procedure 76.3 (Compute and Compare Centrality Measures). When to use: answering “which node matters,” after deciding which sense of “matters” is actually meant.

Decision guide: “how many direct neighbours does this node have?” → degree_centrality. “If this node failed, how much traffic has no alternate route?” → betweenness_centrality. “How much does this node’s importance derive from other important nodes depending on it?” → pagerank.

Procedure 76.4 (Detect Communities and Validate Against Ground Truth). When to use: the graph is suspected to have natural clusters (teams, factions, topic groups) and the goal is to find them from structure alone.

Without ground truth, use nx.community.modularity(G, communities) as a sanity check. Values well above 0 (roughly above 0.3 is a common rule of thumb) suggest real structure.

76.5 Worked Example

The tutorial notebook behind this chapter builds two graphs and measures each of the four operations above on real, printed numbers rather than toy illustrations.

The first is a directed 9-service, 13-edge microservice dependency graph, with a latency_ms weight on every call edge. Running BFS and DFS from api_gateway (Procedure 76.2, bfs_tree/dfs_tree) produces genuinely different visitation orders on the same graph: BFS visits auth_service, user_service, order_service (everything one hop away) before going any deeper, while DFS follows auth_service’s chain all the way to database before backtracking to try user_service.

The shortest-path comparison is the chapter’s clearest concrete point. The fewest-hops path from api_gateway to database is api_gateway -> user_service -> database (2 hops), but its actual latency is 20ms. The lowest-latency path, found by nx.dijkstra_path, is api_gateway -> auth_service -> database, also 2 hops, but only 15ms total. Same hop count, different real cost, because user_service and auth_service have different call latencies to database (12ms vs. 10ms) and different latencies from api_gateway (8ms vs. 5ms). Unweighted shortest path answered a question about hop count; Dijkstra answered the question that actually mattered here.

Centrality (Procedure 76.3) on the same graph, sorted by betweenness:

Service Degree Betweenness PageRank
order_service 0.625 0.071 0.077
notification_service 0.375 0.054 0.076
auth_service 0.250 0.018 0.074
api_gateway 0.375 0.000 0.061
user_service 0.250 0.000 0.082
database 0.625 0.000 0.340
inventory_service 0.250 0.000 0.079
payment_service 0.375 0.000 0.085
shipping_service 0.125 0.000 0.125

database has by far the highest PageRank (0.340) because nearly every service eventually depends on it directly, but its betweenness centrality is 0.0: no shortest path between two other services needs to route through it, because it’s a sink; nothing calls back out of it. order_service, by contrast, has the highest betweenness (0.071) despite tying database on plain degree (0.625): a meaningful share of the graph’s internal shortest paths (to inventory_service, payment_service, notification_service) have no route that avoids it. Picking degree centrality alone would have completely missed this distinction, since order_service and database tie on it. “Everything depends on me” and “a lot of traffic has no way around me” are different claims about different nodes, and a customer asking “what’s our single point of failure?” deserves both answers.

The second graph is Zachary’s Karate Club: a real 1977 friendship network of 34 members and 78 edges that famously split into two real-world factions (“Mr. Hi” and “Officer”) after an actual dispute. Running Louvain community detection (Procedure 76.4) with no faction information at all finds 4 structural communities. Checking those 4 communities’ majority labels against the real split gives 33 of 34 members (97.1%) agreement, a structural, label-free algorithm recovering almost the entire real-world outcome. The one mismatch is not swept under the rug: it reflects a member who really was near-equally connected to both emerging factions, and structural clustering alone can’t always resolve which side a boundary case would end up choosing.

76.6 History and Further Reading

Zachary’s paper “An Information Flow Model for Conflict and Fission in Small Groups” (Zachary 1977) is the source of the karate club network this chapter’s community-detection result is checked against: the original real-world split, not a simulated one. Page and Brin’s paper “The PageRank Citation Ranking” (Page et al. 1998) introduced the recursive-importance algorithm this chapter uses to rank services by how much they’re depended upon. Blondel et al.’s paper “Fast Unfolding of Communities in Large Networks” (Blondel et al. 2008) introduced the Louvain method this chapter’s community detection runs. Book 2, Chapter 31 (RAG & Vector Databases) is the natural point of contrast for this whole track: vector similarity search answers “what reads alike,” and graph traversal answers “what’s connected”: a genuinely different retrieval question, and the reason this track exists.

76.9 Mental Model

A graph algorithm is just a precise question about structure. “What can I reach, and in what order,” “what’s the cheapest route,” “who matters, and in what sense,” “what clusters together” are the same four questions every later chapter in this track keeps re-asking, just against richer graphs (property graphs, knowledge graphs, learned embeddings) and eventually with an LLM doing some of the asking.
