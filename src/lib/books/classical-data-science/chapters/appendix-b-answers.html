Exercise 2.3 [A]. days_since_last_price_increase is safe only if the price increase is known and dated before the prediction point for every row. It can’t be retroactively determined by something that happens after prediction time, such as a price change made in response to early churn signals. If price changes are scheduled independently of individual customer behaviour, the feature is safe. If the business sometimes offers reactive discounts to at-risk customers (which would correlate with the label), it is a leakage risk. It should be excluded, or replaced with a feature computed strictly from data available before the churn-risk window.

Exercise 2.5 [E]. A model routing internal support tickets to one of a few roughly equal-sized teams is a reasonable case for accuracy. Misrouting costs a short reassignment step, not a missed high-stakes event. Classes are close to balanced, and the cost of a false positive/negative is symmetric and low. The imbalance argument specifically targets skewed class distributions with asymmetric costs (fraud, churn, disease), where accuracy can be trivially gamed by always predicting the majority class. Symmetric-cost, balanced-class problems don’t have that failure mode.

Exercise 2.6 [E]. With zero historical failure examples, the checklist fails on “historical data with the label exists”: supervised anomaly detection isn’t viable yet. That builds the labelled dataset needed for a future supervised model rather than pretending one exists today.

Exercise 3.1 [W]. The 99% sensitivity figure describes P(positive∣disease)P(\text{positive} \mid \text{disease})P(positive∣disease), not P(disease∣positive)P(\text{disease} \mid \text{positive})P(disease∣positive). The disease is rare (1% prevalence), so the pool of healthy people is roughly 99 times larger than the pool of diseased people. Even a modest 5% false-positive rate among that huge healthy majority produces far more false positives in absolute terms than the true positives produced by the small diseased minority testing correctly. The 99% figure describes the test’s behaviour conditioned on already knowing someone is sick. It says nothing about what to conclude before knowing that. That’s exactly the direction the diagnosis question runs.

Exercise 4.7 [E]. A reasonable response: memory isn’t the problem. L-BFGS-B keeps only the last few gradient differences, about 10 pairs, so at 50 million parameters it needs roughly 20 vectors × 50M × 8 bytes, about 8 GB. That fits on one GPU. The problem is the gradients. Its 2-iteration speed came from exact, full-batch gradients on a smooth convex problem. A neural network is trained on mini-batches, because the training data rarely fits in memory for a full pass, and mini-batch gradients are noisy. That noise breaks L-BFGS-B’s line search and its curvature estimates. For SGD and Adam the same noise helps, because it pushes the optimiser past saddle points on a non-convex loss surface. So the fast convergence you saw doesn’t transfer, and mini-batch SGD or Adam is the right tool here. The square-size Hessian argument applies to exact Newton’s method, which would need about 4,000 GB (3,725 GiB) at only 1 million parameters. It doesn’t apply to L-BFGS.

Exercise 4.8 [R]. Discussion only. No single answer. Goh’s piece frames momentum’s benefit less as “escaping local minima via averaged-out noise” (the SGD story), and more as correcting for ill-conditioning. When a loss surface curves much more steeply in one direction than another (a narrow, elongated valley), plain gradient descent zig-zags across the narrow axis, while making frustratingly slow progress along the long axis. A single learning rate has to be small enough not to diverge on the steep direction, which caps progress on the shallow one. Momentum’s accumulated velocity partially cancels the oscillation across the narrow axis, since opposing updates average out. It reinforces the consistent direction along the long axis, since aligned updates add up. That’s a curvature-shape argument, distinct from and complementary to the “noise helps escape bad minima” story usually told about SGD. Worth noting the two mechanisms address different pathologies (conditioning vs. non-convexity).

Exercise 5.6 [E]. A correlation of 0.1 and a mutual-information score of 0.1 are not on the same scale, and don’t mean the same thing. Correlation is bounded in [−1,1][-1,1][−1,1], with well-understood conventions for “weak.” Mutual information, in nats, as mutual_info_classif returns it, is unbounded above, and its magnitude depends on the entropy of the specific label and feature distributions in that dataset. A score of 0.1 might be near the noise floor for one problem and a strong, useful signal for another, as the Worked Example showed with feature_noise scoring 0.043 against feature_nonlinear’s 0.338 on the same dataset. The chapter’s Trade-offs section is explicit that MI should only be used to rank features within a single run, not compared against a fixed external threshold. The right move is to always rank features against each other (or against a known-noise baseline feature computed on the same data) rather than picking an absolute cutoff.

Exercise 5.7 [E]. Model answer: a binary label determines which of two shapes a numeric feature is drawn from, both centred at zero. For example, when label is 0, the feature is drawn from a standard normal. When label is 1, the feature is drawn from a mixture that is symmetric around zero but bimodal (e.g., a 50/50 mix of N(−2,0.3)\mathcal{N}(-2, 0.3)N(−2,0.3) and N(2,0.3)\mathcal{N}(2, 0.3)N(2,0.3)). The mean of the feature is approximately zero regardless of the label, so correlation with the label is near zero. But the shape of the distribution, unimodal versus bimodal, differs sharply between labels. That’s exactly the kind of higher-order dependence mutual information, but not correlation, is sensitive to. This is a generalisation of the Worked Example’s variance-based trick to an even more clearly non-linear signal (shape rather than spread).

Exercise 5.8 [R]. Discussion only. No single answer. Shannon’s paper introduces channel capacity and the noisy-channel coding theorem, which this chapter never touches. The idea: a communication channel has a maximum rate at which information can be transmitted with arbitrarily low error, and entropy sets the fundamental limit on lossless compression. Worth considering how this connects to the chapter’s ML framing: a classifier’s cross-entropy loss floor at H(P)H(P)H(P) is structurally the same idea as a channel’s capacity limit. No amount of cleverness can push performance past a limit set by the intrinsic uncertainty of the source. Students should also notice that Shannon’s original motivation was communication engineering, not statistics or machine learning, which came decades later and inherited the same mathematics.

Exercise 7.1 [W]. (a) MCAR: a hardware fault dropping readings at random has no relationship to the reading’s value or any other variable. Exercise 7.4 [A]. Q1 and Q3 are the 25th and 75th percentile of the whole column. Only one value, 312, sits far above the bulk of the data (ages spread across 22–64). Exercise 7.6 [E]. Sample rules: (1) (d['order_total'] > 0).all(), no order should have a non-positive total; (2) (d['discount_pct'] >= 0).all() and (d['discount_pct'] <= 100).all(), a discount percentage must be a valid percentage; (3) d['customer_id'].notnull().all(), every order must be attributable to a customer; (4) a cross-column rule: (d['delivery_date'] >= d['ship_date']).all(), a package cannot be delivered before it ships. Single-column rules can never catch that, since both dates can individually look perfectly valid. A fifth candidate worth adding: (d['ship_date'] - d['order_date']).dt.days.between(0, 30).all() as a sanity bound on fulfilment time, catching data-entry errors that swap order and ship dates.

Exercise 7.7 [R]. Discussion only, no single answer. Multiple imputation with a missingness indicator is the most tractable technique to start with. Alongside the imputed value, an explicit binary column recording whether the value was originally missing is added as a feature. That lets a downstream model learn that “this value was estimated” is itself informative, relevant when missingness correlates with the outcome, as in MNAR. A Heckman correction goes further, formally modelling a “selection equation” for whether a value is observed at all, jointly with the equation of interest. It requires an instrument variable that affects observability but not the outcome directly. That’s often the hardest part to find in practice. Worth considering: unlike MCAR/MAR imputation, none of these techniques make the MNAR problem disappear. They make its assumptions explicit and testable rather than silently violated.

Chapter 8 — SQL for Data Science

Exercise 8.1 [W]. Most SQL engines evaluate HAVING before column aliases defined in the SELECT list are resolved. The aggregate expression SUM(revenue) exists as soon as the grouping happens, but the alias total_revenue is only attached to the output column afterwards, so HAVING can’t yet “see” it. Exercise 8.2 [W]. With three rows tied for first place: ROW_NUMBER() breaks the tie arbitrarily (by whatever order the engine processes ties in) and assigns 1, 2, 3. Every row gets a distinct number even though they’re tied on the ordering column. RANK() assigns all three rows 1, 1, 1 and then the next row after them jumps to 4, a gap appears equal to the number of tied rows. DENSE_RANK() also assigns all three rows 1, 1, 1, but the next row gets 2, no gap. The choice matters operationally. ROW_NUMBER() is right for “give me exactly N rows”: e.g. top 3, no ties allowed. RANK() is right when ties should be visible as ties, with the gap reflecting how many rows share that rank. DENSE_RANK() is right for “how many distinct rank levels are there”: e.g. medal tables, ignoring how many people share a level.

Exercise 8.6 [E]. Exercise 8.7 [E].

SELECT
    category,
    SUM(CASE WHEN status = 'completed'
             THEN revenue ELSE 0 END) AS completed_revenue,
    SUM(CASE WHEN status = 'returned'
             THEN revenue ELSE 0 END) AS returned_revenue
FROM orders o
JOIN products p ON o.product_id = p.product_id
GROUP BY category;

This is preferable to two separate queries joined afterwards. It scans the orders/products join exactly once instead of twice. The database only needs to plan and execute a single query. The result arrives as one row per category, with both figures already aligned. No separate join step is needed in pandas afterwards to line the two result sets up by category. It also guarantees consistency: both numbers are computed from the exact same snapshot of the underlying tables. Two independently-run queries executed moments apart are not strictly guaranteed to be, if the underlying data is being concurrently modified.

Exercise 8.8 [R]. Discussion only, no single answer. The logical evaluation order (FROM → WHERE → GROUP BY → HAVING → window functions → SELECT → ORDER BY) places window functions after WHERE and HAVING. By then, the row set is down to whatever rows and groups survive filtering. Window functions still run before the final SELECT list decides which computed columns to display. That ordering is why a window function can’t appear inside a WHERE clause directly. At the point WHERE is evaluated, no window function has been computed yet. There’s nothing for the filter to reference. It’s also why filtering on a window function’s result requires wrapping the whole query in a CTE or subquery: compute the window function inside, then filter with an outer WHERE. That’s exactly the shape Listing 8.4 and procedures like Procedure 8.5 and the “top-N per group” skeleton in this chapter’s Quick Reference use. Worth connecting this to why HAVING (Exercise 8.1) has the same “can’t reference something not yet computed” character: both restrictions come from the same clause-ordering rule, just applied to aggregates versus window functions respectively.

Exercise 10.6 [E]. Moving from nightly batch scoring to real-time scoring at the moment of a support contact changes all three elements the tutorial’s Stage 6 plan assumed. Chapter 11 — Supervised Learning: Overview of Approaches

Exercise 11.1 [W]. (a) Spam detection using word-count features: Probabilistic (Naive Bayes). Its core assumption is that features are conditionally independent given the class, which is a reasonable approximation for bag-of-words counts given a topic. (b) Predicting house price from square footage and location: Linear. Its core assumption is that the target is well-approximated by a linear combination of the input features. Exercise 11.2 [W]. The equation is Expected Test Error=Bias2+Variance+Irreducible Error\text{Expected Test Error} = \text{Bias}^2 + \text{Variance} + \text{Irreducible Error}Expected Test Error=Bias2+Variance+Irreducible Error. A shallow tree can only express a small number of coarse if/else rules, so it’s forced to represent the true decision boundary poorly no matter what training data it sees. That’s high, stable bias. Letting the tree grow deeper adds more splits, so it can carve out increasingly fine-grained regions that match the true boundary more closely, which is exactly what lowers the bias term. But those extra splits are fit to the specific noise in the training sample, and a different training set would produce different deep splits, so the fitted function becomes unstable across resamples, which is exactly what raises the variance term. Flexibility is a dial on how much the model is allowed to bend to fit what it’s shown. Bending further to reduce systematic error is the same mechanism that makes the fit more sensitive to which particular examples it saw.

In every case the test score sits within one CV fold’s worth of ordinary variation. This ordering tells you almost nothing about overfitting one way or the other. It just means this particular 80/20 test split happened to land on the easy side of average for these four models. A test score meaningfully below the CV mean, or a train/test gap computed directly (Procedure 11.4), is the diagnostic that signals overfitting. A small positive gap like this, even SVM’s larger one, is noise, not evidence of a well-generalising model.

Exercise 11.4 [A]. A Decision Tree builds its predictions entirely out of axis-aligned splits on individual features. Its expressive power comes from chaining many such splits together: each additional feature gives it another axis to cut along, letting it carve out increasingly fine decision regions. Restricted to two features, the tree has nowhere left to turn. It can only ever partition a 2D plane with rectangular cuts, a drastic reduction in what shapes of boundary it can represent. Its accuracy collapses accordingly (0.912 → 0.789). Naive Bayes works completely differently: it multiplies together per-feature likelihoods under an independence assumption, so each feature contributes an essentially separate, additive piece of evidence to the final posterior. Dropping 28 of 30 features just means losing 28 pieces of independent evidence from a sum-like combination. The mechanism itself, multiply what evidence you have, still works exactly the same way with two features as with thirty. It just has less to work with. That’s why Naive Bayes degrades gracefully (0.930 → 0.877) while the tree, which fundamentally needs many features to build its structure, degrades sharply.

Exercise 11.5 [E]. Rule out K-Nearest Neighbours first. The Trade-offs table already flags KNN as “slow at predict time,” and the Worked Example gives the concrete number: 35.4 ms to serve predictions on the held-out test set of only 114 samples drawn from a 455-row training set. That’s six times slower than the next-slowest algorithm and over sixty times slower than the fastest. KNN’s prediction cost is O(n)O(n)O(n) in the size of the training set (per Listing 11.1, every prediction requires a distance computation against every stored training point). Scaling from 455 to 40,000 training rows makes that cost far worse, not better. The per-request latency would grow roughly in proportion to the extra data, blowing well past the 5 ms budget, even before accounting for the nonlinear-interaction requirement working against a distance metric already strained by many features. Random Forest or gradient-boosted trees, which handle nonlinear interactions natively and predict in single-digit milliseconds regardless of training set size, are the algorithms worth trying here.

Exercise 11.6 [E]. “No algorithm is better than any other” implies a working data scientist should be indifferent between models. That is false and dangerous advice in practice. On the concentric-circles dataset, Logistic Regression scored 0.289, worse than a coin flip, while Random Forest scored a perfect 1.000, on the same data. Two algorithms performed wildly differently on one concrete problem. Nothing about that result says “they’re equally good on average across every problem.” But it emphatically says they are not equally good on this problem, which is the one the data scientist has to solve. The narrower claim the theorem makes: averaged over all possible problems, including ones with no structure whatsoever, unlike anything encountered in practice, no algorithm has an edge. It says nothing about the much smaller, highly structured set of problems real datasets belong to, where algorithms whose inductive bias matches the data’s actual structure, a linear model on linearly-separable blobs, a tree ensemble on nonlinearly-separable circles, reliably win.

Exercise 11.7 [R]. Discussion only, no single answer. Applying Burkov’s framework to Naive Bayes: the hypothesis representation is a set of per-feature conditional probability distributions, one likelihood distribution per feature per class (for GaussianNB, a Gaussian mean and variance for each numeric feature within each class). These are combined multiplicatively via Bayes’ theorem to produce a posterior over classes. The loss function is essentially absent in the sense logistic regression uses one. Rather than defining an error to minimise and searching for parameters that reduce it, Naive Bayes’ parameters are the maximum-likelihood estimates of each per-feature distribution, computed directly from counts or sample statistics. The optimisation procedure follows from that. The independence assumption reduces parameter estimation to closed-form per-feature statistics (a mean and variance per class, or a frequency count per category). So fitting is a single pass over the training data with no iteration. Contrast Logistic Regression, whose sigmoid-linked linear combination has no closed-form maximum-likelihood solution and requires an iterative convex optimiser (gradient descent or L-BFGS, controlled by max_iter) that repeatedly evaluates and adjusts every coefficient until convergence. That structural difference, closed-form counting versus iterative search, is exactly why Naive Bayes trains in under 2 ms while Logistic Regression takes over 15 ms on the same data, nearly a 10x gap. The gap widens sharply as feature counts grow.

Chapter 12 — Ensemble Methods

Exercise 12.1 [W]. If BBB models each have variance σ2\sigma^2σ2 and their errors are uncorrelated, averaging them gives variance σ2/B\sigma^2/Bσ2/B, strictly smaller as BBB grows, with no term in that formula affecting bias. Bagging doesn’t change what each individual model is trying to predict: no change to the systematic error, i.e. bias. It only reduces how much the ensemble’s average prediction fluctuates around that target across different training samples.

Exercise 12.2 [W]. (a) Boosting: a model systematically wrong on a subgroup has high bias on that subgroup, which boosting directly targets by reweighting/refitting on exactly the hard cases. Bagging’s averaging effect plateaus once the ensemble is large enough to cancel variance. Exercise 13.3 [A]. The ten seed-accuracy values have a standard deviation of about 0.010 (np.std), roughly 1 percentage point. That’s somewhat larger than the 5-fold CV standard deviation of 0.007. The 5-fold CV estimate is still the more trustworthy of the two, and not just because its std happens to be smaller. It’s built from five disjoint folds that together cover the whole dataset exactly once, systematically. The ten single-split values, by contrast, are ten separate 80/20 splits that overlap heavily. They aren’t independent measurements of the same underlying quantity, not in a structured resampling sense. A single seed’s score depends entirely on which specific examples happened to land in that one test set. CV’s reported score is already an average over five such sets. Exercise 18.6 [E]. The feature in the thousands will have its weight naturally driven small by ordinary least-squares fitting, to keep its large-magnitude contribution to predictions reasonable. The L2/L1 penalty, which acts on raw weight magnitude, barely touches it as a result. The 0-to-1-scaled feature’s weight, meanwhile, will naturally be larger to have any effect at all, so it absorbs a disproportionate share of the regularisation penalty. The practical effect: the large-scale feature is under-regularised, and the small-scale feature is over-regularised, purely as an artefact of scale rather than actual relevance. That’s exactly why scaling must always precede regularisation.

Exercise 18.7 [R]. Discussion only. In the Gaussian-prior interpretation, the prior’s variance σ2\sigma^2σ2 and Ridge’s α\alphaα are inversely related. A smaller prior variance expresses stronger belief that weights should be close to zero, which mathematically corresponds to a larger penalty (larger α\alphaα) pulling the MAP (maximum a posteriori) estimate towards zero more forcefully. A very small prior variance is a strong prior belief in small weights, exactly mirroring a large α\alphaα’s strong regularisation pressure.

Chapter 19 — Unsupervised Learning

Exercise 19.1 [W]. K-Means’s algorithm requires KKK centroids to initialise and assign points to before it can even begin iterating. The number of centroids is a structural input to the procedure, not something it discovers. DBSCAN instead defines clusters implicitly, as whatever dense regions emerge from the eps/min_samples density criteria applied to the actual data. However many dense regions exist is however many clusters result, with no upfront count required.

Exercise 19.2 [W]. (a) K-Means: large, roughly spherical, well-separated segments are exactly K-Means’s best-case assumption, and it scales better than DBSCAN on large data. (b) DBSCAN: an unknown cluster count with irregular shapes and genuine noise is exactly the case K-Means structurally cannot handle (fixed K, spherical assumption, no built-in outlier concept) and DBSCAN is built for.

Exercise 19.3 [A]. Silhouette score measures how well each point fits its assigned cluster relative to the next-nearest cluster, using whatever clustering was produced. It has no access to the true crescent-shaped structure, and cannot detect that K-Means drew the wrong kind of boundary. A K-Means clustering on the moons data could report a moderate-to-decent silhouette score for the (wrong) roughly-circular split it found. That’s precisely because silhouette only asks “is this point closer to its own cluster’s centroid than to the other one,” which K-Means’s own circular boundaries can satisfy reasonably well even while completely missing the true crescent shapes. Silhouette validates internal consistency with the algorithm’s own assumptions, not correctness against ground truth.

Exercise 19.5 [E]. “How well do those 6 clusters hold up when you show them to someone who knows these customers? Do the groups correspond to real, distinct behaviours, or just to a geometrically tidy split of the feature space? A good silhouette score tells you the clusters are internally compact and well-separated; it says nothing about whether they’re the useful 6 groups for your business decisions.”

Exercise 19.6 [E]. Setting contamination to whatever value produces the “cleanest-looking” separation is circular, because it’s using the model’s own output to justify the input that produced that output. Instead of contamination reflecting an actual prior belief about how much of the data is truly anomalous, it’s being reverse-engineered from a desired visual result. This risks hiding real anomalies (if the “clean” contamination is set too low), or manufacturing fake ones (if set too high), rather than reflecting genuine fraud prevalence.

Exercise 19.7 [R]. Discussion only. The Isolation Forest paper’s key complexity claim is that it achieves roughly linear time complexity and constant memory with respect to dataset size. That’s because it doesn’t need to compute pairwise distances or density estimates the way distance-based methods (like LOF or One-Class SVM) do: isolation only requires random splits, not distance comparisons between all pairs of points. This directly explains this chapter’s “scales well, works in high dimensions” summary. Avoiding pairwise distance computation is exactly what lets it scale where density- or distance-based alternatives struggle.

Chapter 20 — Time Series Analysis

Exercise 20.1 [W]. Earlier chapters’ datasets were assumed independent and identically distributed. Each row’s order carries no information, so shuffling before splitting doesn’t change what the model can learn. Time series data has the opposite property by definition: today’s value depends on yesterday’s. A random split lets some training rows sit chronologically after some test rows, letting the model implicitly “see” future information relative to what it’s being scored on.

Exercise 20.2 [W]. A slowly-decaying ACF (no sharp cutoff) is the signature of non-stationarity: the series’ statistical properties are drifting over time rather than settling into a stable pattern. The action it suggests is differencing the series (and re-running the ADF test) before attempting to fit ACF/PACF-based model orders or an ARIMA model at all.

Exercise 20.4 [A]. Naive MAE (4.602) is lower than ARIMA MAE (7.228) by (7.228−4.602)/7.228≈36.3%(7.228-4.602)/7.228 \approx 36.3\%(7.228−4.602)/7.228≈36.3%: naive outperforms ARIMA by roughly 36% on this metric. ARIMA’s explicit trend/autoregressive structure has more room to demonstrate a genuine advantage once the naive baseline’s flat-line assumption clearly breaks down.

Exercise 20.6 [E]. Computing .rolling(7).mean() without .shift(1) first means the rolling window for row ttt includes row ttt’s own value. The feature meant to summarise “the past 7 days” includes today’s target value baked into its own input. That’s a direct form of leakage: the model gets to see (an average containing) the answer as part of its own input feature.

Exercise 20.7 [R]. Discussion only. Hyndman and Athanasopoulos discuss measures like MASE (Mean Absolute Scaled Error), which scales errors relative to a naive/seasonal-naive baseline’s own error rather than relative to the raw target values. It’s designed specifically to allow comparison across series of very different scales, and to remain well-behaved when the series can be at or near zero, exactly the situation this chapter flags as breaking MAPE.
