Chapter 3 gave linear regression a closed-form solution: the normal equations solve for the exact optimum in one step. The underlying loss surface is a simple quadratic bowl, with a single minimum reachable by matrix algebra. That trick doesn’t generalise. Logistic regression, neural networks, and nearly every other model this book covers have no closed-form solution. They have to be found by search, starting from a random guess and iteratively improving it until the loss is small enough. This chapter is about that search. What a gradient is. Why walking downhill on a loss surface finds good parameters. Why step size matters so much. Why the chain rule of calculus, applied billions of times per training run, is the entire mechanism behind training a neural network. Everything from logistic regression in Chapter 11 through fine-tuning language models later in this series runs on the ideas in this chapter.

4.1 Motivation

Training a model is optimisation: find the parameters that minimise a loss function measuring how wrong the model’s predictions are. The loss function defines a surface over parameter space. For a model with two weights, imagine literal terrain, with height at each point equal to the loss for those two weight values.

Training is the search for the lowest point in that terrain. Gradient descent is the specific search algorithm this book relies on almost everywhere: stand where you are, work out which direction goes downhill steepest, take a step, repeat. It scales from a single-parameter toy problem to models with hundreds of billions of parameters. That’s why it, not exact algebraic solutions, is the workhorse of modern machine learning.

4.2 Exposition

4.2.1 Derivatives and Gradients

A derivative measures a function’s rate of change at a point. You already have an intuition for this from somewhere far more familiar than calculus class. Velocity is the derivative of position with respect to time: how fast something is moving, right now. Acceleration is the derivative of velocity with respect to time: how fast the speed itself is changing. A car’s speedometer is displaying a derivative in real time, and pressing the accelerator changes a derivative of a derivative. For a function f(x)f(x)f(x), the derivative dfdx\frac{df}{dx}dxdf​ evaluated at xxx asks the same question in general form: if xxx increases by a tiny amount, how much does fff change, and in which direction? The sign carries the information that matters for optimisation. If dfdx>0\frac{df}{dx} > 0dxdf​>0, fff is increasing at xxx, so decreasing xxx reduces fff. If dfdx<0\frac{df}{dx} < 0dxdf​<0, the reverse. If dfdx=0\frac{df}{dx} = 0dxdf​=0, the function is momentarily flat, a candidate minimum, maximum, or saddle point.

Real loss functions depend on many parameters at once: one weight per feature, easily thousands in a modest model. The multivariate generalisation of the derivative is the gradient, ∇f\nabla f∇f, the vector of partial derivatives, one per parameter:

∇f(w)=[∂f∂w1,∂f∂w2,…,∂f∂wn]\nabla f(\mathbf{w}) = \left[ \frac{\partial f}{\partial w_1}, \frac{\partial f}{\partial w_2}, \ldots, \frac{\partial f}{\partial w_n} \right]∇f(w)=[∂w1​∂f​,∂w2​∂f​,…,∂wn​∂f​]

Each entry says how the loss responds to nudging that one parameter while holding the others fixed. The gradient vector as a whole points in the direction of steepest ascent of fff. To reduce the loss, step in the opposite direction. That’s the basis of gradient descent.

4.2.2 Gradient Descent

The update rule follows directly from the previous section:

w←w−α ∇f(w)\mathbf{w} \leftarrow \mathbf{w} - \alpha \, \nabla f(\mathbf{w})w←w−α∇f(w)

where w\mathbf{w}w is the current parameter vector, α\alphaα (the learning rate) controls how large a step to take, and ∇f(w)\nabla f(\mathbf{w})∇f(w) is the gradient of the loss at the current parameters. Repeating this update (recompute the gradient, take a step, recompute, take another step) is gradient descent. It ends, in practice, when the loss stops decreasing meaningfully (convergence), or after a fixed number of iterations.

Nothing in this rule requires knowing the shape of the whole loss surface in advance. Gradient descent only ever needs the local slope at the current position. That’s exactly why it scales to problems too large to visualize or solve algebraically.

4.2.3 The Learning Rate

The learning rate α\alphaα governs a trade-off in every iterative optimiser, and there’s no universal right answer for it.

Too large, and each step overshoots the minimum. The parameters bounce past the bottom of the bowl, land higher up the opposite wall. The loss oscillates or diverges outright, getting worse with each iteration instead of better. Too small, and steps barely move the parameters. Training still converges eventually. But it may take orders of magnitude more iterations, and so far more compute and wall-clock time, to get there. Just right produces steady, roughly monotonic loss decrease that levels off near a minimum.

NoteWhat Does Monotonic Mean?

A sequence is monotonic if it only moves in one direction: always decreasing, or always increasing, never doubling back. A “roughly monotonic” loss curve mostly falls, step after step, without bouncing up, then down, then up again along the way. It doesn’t have to fall by the same amount every step, just not reverse direction for long.

Because the correct value depends on the specific problem, the architecture, and the scale of the input data, practitioners rarely fix a single learning rate for an entire run. Learning rate schedules, which reduce α\alphaα over the course of training, are standard practice because “just right” tends to shrink as training approaches convergence.

4.2.4 The Chain Rule and Backpropagation

Neural networks are compositions of functions. The output of one layer is the input to the next. Computing the gradient of the loss with respect to a weight buried several layers deep requires the chain rule from calculus:

NoteThe Chain Rule, in Plain Words

Neural network layers are functions stacked inside each other: the loss depends on the last layer’s output, which depends on the layer before that, and so on, all the way back to the first weight. The chain rule is just the rule for how a nudge ripples through a chain like that. Picture a set of gears. Turn the first gear, and it turns the second one at some ratio. The second turns the third at another ratio. How fast the last gear spins, for one turn of the first, is just those ratios multiplied straight through the chain. A derivative is a local ratio like that: how much the output moves for a small nudge to the input. Chaining functions together, f(g(x))f(g(x))f(g(x)), chains their local ratios together the same way the gears do. That’s the whole content of “the derivative of f(g(x))f(g(x))f(g(x)) with respect to xxx.” ggg is the inner gear, fff is the outer one. Their local rates of change multiply to give the rate of change of the whole chain.

ddx[f(g(x))]=f′(g(x))⋅g′(x)\frac{d}{dx}\big[f(g(x))\big] = f'(g(x)) \cdot g'(x)dxd​[f(g(x))]=f′(g(x))⋅g′(x)

Applied repeatedly, layer by layer, from the loss backward to the earliest weights, this is backpropagation. It propagates the gradient of the loss backward through a network, multiplying by each layer’s local derivative along the way. Frameworks like PyTorch and TensorFlow automate this via autograd, tracking every operation done on a tensor and replaying the chain rule automatically to compute exact gradients. But the chain rule is the mechanism underneath the automation. Understanding it is what turns autograd from magic into an implementation detail.

4.2.5 Loss Functions

A loss function maps predictions and true labels to a single scalar: the number gradient descent is trying to minimise. The choice of loss function defines what “good” means for a given model. This is a modelling decision to be made as you design your solution.

Mean Squared Error (MSE), for regression:

L=1N∑i=1N(y^i−yi)2L = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} (\hat{y}_i - y_i)^2L=N1​i=1∑N​(y^​i​−yi​)2

Squaring penalises large errors disproportionately and produces a smooth function that is differentiable everywhere, convenient for gradient-based optimisation.

Binary cross-entropy, for binary classification:

L=−1N∑i=1N[yilog⁡(y^i)+(1−yi)log⁡(1−y^i)]L = -\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \big[y_i \log(\hat{y}_i) + (1-y_i)\log(1-\hat{y}_i)\big]L=−N1​i=1∑N​[yi​log(y^​i​)+(1−yi​)log(1−y^​i​)]

This isn’t arbitrary. The ingredients aren’t new. It falls directly out of maximum likelihood estimation under the Bernoulli distribution from Chapter 3. It is zero for a perfect prediction and grows without bound as a confident prediction diverges from the truth.

Mean Absolute Error (MAE), for regression that needs to be robust to outliers:

L=1N∑i=1N∣y^i−yi∣L = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} |\hat{y}_i - y_i|L=N1​i=1∑N​∣y^​i​−yi​∣

MAE weighs every error linearly rather than quadratically. A handful of extreme outliers can’t dominate the loss the way they can under MSE. But it is not differentiable at zero, which forces optimisers to fall back on subgradients there.

4.2.6 Variants of Gradient Descent

The update rule above leaves an open question: the gradient of the loss over how much data, computed at each step? Three answers are used in practice, trading off gradient accuracy against speed.

Batch gradient descent computes the gradient over the entire training set before taking a single step. This is the most accurate gradient available. But for large datasets, one step becomes prohibitively slow to compute.

Stochastic gradient descent (SGD) computes the gradient from a single example at a time. Each individual step is nearly free to compute, but noisy. An update based on one example may not point in the true downhill direction at all. Counterintuitively, that noise isn’t always negative: it can help the optimiser escape shallow local minima that a smoother trajectory would settle into.

Mini-batch gradient descent computes the gradient over a small batch of examples, typically 32, 64, 128, or 256 at a time. This is the practical compromise used throughout neural network training. Batches are large enough to average out most of the single-example noise. Small enough to keep each step fast. And a natural fit for GPU parallelism.

4.2.7 Momentum and Adam

Plain gradient descent treats every step independently, with no memory of where it has already been. That makes it slow to cross flat regions, and prone to zig-zagging in narrow, curved valleys. Two refinements address this.

Momentum accumulates a running velocity that blends the new gradient with the previous step’s direction:

v←βv+(1−β)∇f(w),w←w−αv\mathbf{v} \leftarrow \beta \mathbf{v} + (1-\beta)\nabla f(\mathbf{w}), \qquad \mathbf{w} \leftarrow \mathbf{w} - \alpha \mathbf{v}v←βv+(1−β)∇f(w),w←w−αv

Consistent gradient directions reinforce each other and accelerate. Oscillating directions partially cancel and dampen. The effect is qualitatively like a ball rolling downhill, carrying momentum through small bumps rather than reacting to every local wiggle.

Adam (Adaptive Moment Estimation) goes further, combining momentum with a per-parameter adaptive learning rate. It tracks both a running average of the gradient (like momentum) and a running average of the squared gradient. Then it scales each parameter’s step inversely with the typical magnitude of that parameter’s recent gradients. Parameters with consistently large gradients get smaller effective steps. Parameters with small gradients get larger ones. The practical consequence is that Adam is less sensitive to the initial choice of learning rate than SGD. That’s why it is the default optimiser for training neural networks.

4.2.8 When scipy Can Help, and When It Can’t

For classical, low-dimensional optimisation problems (fitting a curve, tuning a handful of hyperparameters, minimising a smooth well-behaved function), scipy.optimize.minimize provides solvers that are faster and more reliable than a hand-rolled gradient descent loop. It is the right tool for hyperparameter search, fitting statistical models, and any smooth optimisation with at most a few hundred to a few thousand parameters.

It is not a substitute for mini-batch gradient descent when training a neural network, for three reasons. The parameter space is enormous: millions to billions of dimensions. scipy’s more powerful solvers approximate second-order curvature information, a matrix whose size grows with the square of the parameter count. That becomes computationally infeasible at that scale. Mini-batch stochasticity is not noise we want to eliminate. It is how these optimisers escape saddle points that riddle non-convex loss surfaces. And realistic training data rarely fits in memory all at once anyway. Batch-by-batch processing is required regardless of which optimiser is used.

4.3 Trade-offs and Limits

Convex versus non-convex loss surfaces. For a convex loss function (logistic regression’s is one example), gradient descent is guaranteed to find the single global minimum. A convex bowl has no other minima to get stuck in. Neural network loss surfaces are not convex. They are riddled with local minima and saddle points. Gradient descent finds a good minimum, not provably the best one.

Recall the terrain from this chapter’s Motivation: for a model with two weights, height at each point is loss. A convex loss function is that terrain shaped like a single bowl, one valley, one lowest point, no way to get stuck anywhere else. A non-convex loss function is terrain like this instead:

Gradient descent dropped into the shallow valley on the right has no way to know the deeper one exists. Every direction from its floor goes up, so it stops, converged, and wrong about being done. That is what “riddled with local minima and saddle points” looks like on real terrain, not just as a phrase.

In practice, for large neural networks, the local minima found this way tend to generalise well regardless. This is an empirical observation about large models. Not a theorem.

First-order versus second-order methods. Gradient descent uses only first derivatives, the slope. Newton’s method and its relatives also use second derivatives: curvature, captured in the Hessian matrix. That lets them converge in far fewer iterations near a minimum. The catch is that the Hessian is an M×MM \times MM×M matrix for MMM parameters, so storing and inverting it becomes infeasible before MMM reaches the billions-to-trillions scale of modern neural networks. This is why first-order adaptive methods like Adam, not second-order methods, dominate deep learning. The trade-off is convergence speed per step, against whether the method can run at all. At GenAI scale that’s barely a contest: second-order methods can’t run at that size to begin with.

4.4 Procedures

Procedure 4.1 (Choose a Starting Learning Rate). When to use: at the start of any training run, before the first real epoch.

Common mistake: picking a learning rate once and never revisiting it. A rate that is “just right” early in training is frequently too large once the loss has mostly converged, which is the problem learning rate schedules (Procedure 4.4) exist to solve.

Procedure 4.2 (Read a Loss Curve). When to use: after every training run, as the first diagnostic before touching any hyperparameter.

Procedure 4.3 (Use scipy.optimize for Classical Optimisation). When to use: low-dimensional optimisation (well under 1,000 parameters), curve fitting, or whenever a precise minimum matters more than raw training speed.

Common mistake: reaching for scipy.optimize.minimize on a neural network because “it converges faster” on small problems. See this chapter’s Exposition on why the Hessian-based methods scipy favours do not scale past a few thousand parameters.

Procedure 4.4 (Schedule a Decaying Learning Rate). When to use: training runs long enough that a single fixed learning rate is a poor fit across the whole run.

Procedure 4.5 (Spot Vanishing or Exploding Gradients). When to use: a neural network’s loss refuses to decrease, or diverges outright, and the learning rate alone doesn’t explain it.

Common mistake: treating every diverging loss as a learning-rate problem. A loss that goes to NaN after training stably for many epochs is more often exploding gradients deep in the network than a suddenly-wrong learning rate. Check gradient norms (Listing 4.5) before cutting α\alphaα.

4.10 Mental Model

Training a model is hiking downhill in darkness. You can’t see the whole landscape. Only which way is downhill from where you’re currently standing. Gradient descent is the algorithm for taking a step based on that local slope. The learning rate is how big a step to take. Adam adds a kind of adaptive GPS, scaling each direction’s step size based on how steep that particular direction has been recently. The chain rule is what makes it possible to compute “which way is downhill,” even when the loss is buried many layers deep inside a composed function. That’s exactly what a neural network is.
