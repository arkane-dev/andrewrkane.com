Why does classification use a logarithm in its loss function? Why do decision trees split on “information gain”? Why does a variational autoencoder’s loss include a strange penalty term called KL divergence? Why does RLHF constrain a fine-tuned model with something by the same name? All four questions collapse to a single answer: information theory. The mathematics of uncertainty. It gives a precise vocabulary for how much a distribution doesn’t know, how expensive a wrong prediction is, and how far apart two probability distributions sit from each other. This chapter builds that vocabulary from a single equation up through four concepts (entropy, cross-entropy, KL divergence, and mutual information) that recur constantly for the rest of this book, usually without ever being named.

5.1 Motivation

Every concept in this chapter comes from one idea: how “surprising” is an event? Flipping a fair coin and getting heads is unsurprising. Flipping an unfair coin that almost always lands heads and getting tails is very surprising. Claude Shannon formalised this in 1948 with the information content (or self-information) of an event with probability ppp:

I(p)=−log⁡2(p)I(p) = -\log_2(p)I(p)=−log2​(p)

Low-probability events carry high information content: they’re surprising. High-probability events carry low information content: they’re expected. Entropy is the average of this quantity over a whole distribution. Cross-entropy and KL divergence compare two distributions using the same log-probability machinery. Mutual information asks how much one variable’s distribution shifts when another is known. This is one idea, viewed from four angles. Those four angles happen to be exactly the four things a data scientist needs when training and evaluating models.

5.2 Exposition

5.2.1 Entropy: How Uncertain Is a Distribution?

Entropy, written H(P)H(P)H(P), is the average information content of a distribution. For a discrete distribution PPP over KKK outcomes:

H(P)=−∑xP(x)log⁡P(x)H(P) = -\sum_{x} P(x) \log P(x)H(P)=−x∑​P(x)logP(x)

Entropy measures uncertainty, not correctness. It says nothing about whether a distribution is right, only how spread out it is. Bounded by two extremes. At maximum entropy, the distribution is uniform: with KKK classes, H(P)=log⁡(K)H(P) = \log(K)H(P)=log(K). There’s no information favouring any one outcome over another. At minimum entropy, H(P)=0H(P) = 0H(P)=0: a point mass, one outcome with probability 1. Zero uncertainty.

This is why decision trees split on entropy (or the closely related Gini impurity, discussed below). At a tree node, entropy measures how mixed the classes are. A good split is one that produces child nodes with lower combined entropy than the parent. Those children are more predictable, closer to a point mass, than the node they came from. The information gain of a split is the entropy reduction it gives.

NoteWhat Is Gini Impurity?

Gini impurity measures the same thing entropy does, how mixed a set of classes is, but with a different formula:

Gini(P)=1−∑xP(x)2\text{Gini}(P) = 1 - \sum_x P(x)^2Gini(P)=1−x∑​P(x)2

Picture picking one example at random from a node and labelling it with a random guess drawn from the node’s own class proportions. Gini impurity is the probability that guess is wrong. A pure node (every example the same class) has Gini impurity 0. Nobody could guess wrong there. A node split evenly across two classes has Gini impurity 0.5, a coin flip. Like entropy, it bottoms out at a point mass and peaks when classes are evenly mixed. The Trade-offs section below compares the two.

5.2.2 Cross-Entropy: The Cost of Being Wrong

Cross-entropy measures how well a predicted distribution QQQ approximates a true distribution PPP:

H(P,Q)=−∑xP(x)log⁡Q(x)H(P, Q) = -\sum_{x} P(x) \log Q(x)H(P,Q)=−x∑​P(x)logQ(x)

When Q=PQ = PQ=P (the model predicts exactly right), cross-entropy equals entropy, its minimum possible value. When QQQ diverges from PPP, cross-entropy grows. It grows in a specific, asymmetric way: the model pays a small penalty for being uncertain, and a large penalty for being confidently wrong.

This is the loss function used to train classifiers. In binary classification, PPP is the true label (0 or 1) and QQQ is the model’s predicted probability y^\hat yy^​. The binary cross-entropy loss is:

L=−[ylog⁡(y^)+(1−y)log⁡(1−y^)]L = -\big[y \log(\hat y) + (1 - y) \log(1 - \hat y)\big]L=−[ylog(y^​)+(1−y)log(1−y^​)]

When y^\hat yy^​ is close to yyy, LLL is small. When y^\hat yy^​ is far from yyy (say y=1y = 1y=1 but y^=0.01\hat y = 0.01y^​=0.01), log⁡(0.01)\log(0.01)log(0.01) is a large negative number and LLL explodes. Cross-entropy punishes confident wrong predictions far more heavily than uncertain ones. That’s the behaviour wanted from a classification loss: a model that says “I’m 99% sure this is fraud” and is wrong should be penalised much harder than one that shrugged and said “meh, maybe, 50/50.”

Cross-entropy and entropy are linked by an identity that matters more than you might think (if you’re new to this, you’d have no reason to think it matters at all):

H(P,Q)=H(P)+KL(P ∥ Q)H(P, Q) = H(P) + \mathrm{KL}(P \,\|\, Q)H(P,Q)=H(P)+KL(P∥Q)

Cross-entropy equals the true entropy plus a penalty term (KL divergence) for using the wrong distribution. H(P)H(P)H(P), the entropy of the true labels, is fixed. It doesn’t depend on the model’s parameters. So minimising cross-entropy during training is mathematically identical to minimising KL divergence between the model’s predictions and the truth.

5.2.3 KL Divergence: How Far Is Q From P?

KL divergence (Kullback-Leibler divergence) is a directed measure of how different a distribution QQQ is from a reference distribution PPP:

KL(P ∥ Q)=∑xP(x)log⁡P(x)Q(x)\mathrm{KL}(P \,\|\, Q) = \sum_{x} P(x) \log \frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}KL(P∥Q)=x∑​P(x)logQ(x)P(x)​

Three properties matter in practice. First, KL(P ∥ Q)≥0\mathrm{KL}(P \,\|\, Q) \geq 0KL(P∥Q)≥0 always, with equality only when P=QP = QP=Q. It behaves somewhat like a distance. Second, and unlike a true distance, it is not symmetric: KL(P ∥ Q)≠KL(Q ∥ P)\mathrm{KL}(P \,\|\, Q) \neq \mathrm{KL}(Q \,\|\, P)KL(P∥Q)=KL(Q∥P) in general. It’s a directed divergence, not a metric. Third, it is undefined (infinite) at any point where Q(x)=0Q(x) = 0Q(x)=0 but P(x)>0P(x) > 0P(x)>0. QQQ must cover the support of PPP, or the divergence blows up.

KL divergence shows up constantly beyond the classification loss it’s hiding inside. A variational autoencoder’s loss includes a KL term that pulls the learned latent distribution towards something close to a standard Gaussian. RLHF constrains a fine-tuned language model with a KL penalty against the base model. That keeps optimisation for the reward signal from drifting the model into degenerate, reward-hacking behaviour. Knowledge distillation trains a small student model to minimise its KL divergence from a large teacher model’s output distribution. TRPO, and the KL-penalty variant of PPO, use KL divergence between successive policies to keep updates from being too large. In every one of these, the same asymmetric, always-non-negative quantity from the equation above is doing the work.

NoteWhat Is a Latent Distribution?

A latent variable is one the model never observes directly. It’s the model’s own internal, compressed summary of the input, learned rather than measured. A variational autoencoder compresses each input (an image, say) down to a small vector of numbers, its latent representation. It then tries to reconstruct the original input from just that vector. The latent distribution is the probability distribution over those compressed vectors that the model learns to produce. Pulling it towards a standard Gaussian, via the KL term mentioned above, keeps that compressed space well-behaved: smooth, centred, and usable for generating new examples by sampling from it later. Book 2 covers variational autoencoders and their architecture in full.

5.2.4 Mutual Information: How Much Does Knowing X Tell You About Y?

Mutual information measures how much knowing one variable reduces uncertainty about another:

I(X;Y)=H(X)−H(X∣Y)=H(Y)−H(Y∣X)I(X; Y) = H(X) - H(X \mid Y) = H(Y) - H(Y \mid X)I(X;Y)=H(X)−H(X∣Y)=H(Y)−H(Y∣X)

Equivalently, I(X;Y)=KL(P(X,Y) ∥ P(X)P(Y))I(X;Y) = \mathrm{KL}\big(P(X,Y) \,\|\, P(X)P(Y)\big)I(X;Y)=KL(P(X,Y)∥P(X)P(Y)): mutual information is the KL divergence between the actual joint distribution of XXX and YYY and the joint distribution they would have if independent. When I(X;Y)=0I(X;Y) = 0I(X;Y)=0, XXX and YYY are independent: knowing one tells you nothing about the other. When I(X;Y)I(X;Y)I(X;Y) is large, they’re strongly dependent.

The practical use is feature selection:
sklearn.feature_selection.mutual_info_classif ranks candidate features by how much each reduces uncertainty about the label. Chapter 3 built correlation and covariance out of sums of products of deviations from the mean. That machinery only ever sees straight-line relationships. Mutual information asks the same practical question, does this feature relate to the label, but answers it from entropy instead of deviations. That different foundation is exactly why it succeeds where correlation fails. Nothing in its construction assumes the relationship is linear, so it catches dependence covariance is structurally blind to. Mutual information also underlies the information bottleneck framework, for understanding what a neural network’s internal layers retain. It is also behind contrastive methods like SimCLR and CLIP. Their InfoNCE loss maximises a lower bound on the mutual information between paired inputs: two augmented views of one image (SimCLR), or an image and its caption (CLIP).

Entropy is the baseline difficulty of a problem. Cross-entropy is what a model is being scored on. KL divergence is the gap between the two. Mutual information asks the same underlying question (how much does one thing tell you about another) of two variables rather than of a model against the truth.

5.3 Trade-offs and Limits

Entropy and Gini impurity are the two candidates for measuring node impurity in decision trees. In practice they produce nearly identical splits. Entropy is more theoretically grounded: it’s the same quantity used everywhere else in this chapter. Gini is a little cheaper to compute, since it needs no logarithm, and scikit-learn defaults to it. The choice rarely changes which feature a tree picks.

Cross-entropy versus mean squared error for classification is not such a close call. Cross-entropy is the mathematically correct loss: it falls directly out of the likelihood of a Bernoulli or categorical distribution. MSE applied to classification produces saturated gradients near 0 and 1, exactly where a classifier most needs strong gradient signal to fix a confident, wrong prediction. Cross-entropy should always be used for classification. Reaching for MSE there is a common and avoidable mistake.

KL divergence’s asymmetry could be seen as a flaw. Better to treat it as a feature to be deliberate about. KL(P||Q) and KL(Q||P) answer different questions. Mixing them up produces subtly wrong regularisation or drift-detection behaviour. Always be explicit about which distribution is “truth” (the first argument) and which is the “approximation” (the second). Mutual information’s main limitation is that, unlike a correlation coefficient’s fixed [−1,1][-1, 1][−1,1] range, it has no fixed upper bound and no universal “this counts as strong” threshold. It is safe to use for ranking features against each other within one dataset and one run, but not as an absolute quality score comparable across different problems.

5.4 Procedures

Procedure 5.1 (Compute Entropy of a Distribution). When to use: summarising how uncertain or spread out a distribution is: class balance in a dataset, or a model’s predicted class probabilities.

Common mistake: passing raw counts instead of a normalised distribution. entropy([90, 10]) is wrong. Normalise first with p / p.sum(), or let scipy.stats.entropy do it (it normalises automatically, unlike a hand-rolled version).

Procedure 5.2 (Compute Cross-Entropy Loss). When to use: training or evaluating a classifier, or sanity-checking predicted probabilities against ground truth.

Here is a rule of thumb for reading raw loss values (natural log, nats). Around 0.69 is no better than a coin flip on a balanced binary problem (−log⁡(0.5)-\log(0.5)−log(0.5)); below 0.1 is confident and mostly correct; above 2.0 means the model is confidently wrong on a meaningful fraction of examples.

Common mistake: feeding raw logits into a loss function that expects probabilities, or vice versa. sklearn.metrics.log_loss expects probabilities in [0,1][0, 1][0,1]. PyTorch’s nn.CrossEntropyLoss expects raw logits and applies softmax internally. Passing already-softmaxed probabilities double-applies the transform and silently produces a plausible-looking but wrong loss.

Procedure 5.3 (Compute KL Divergence Between Two Distributions). When to use: comparing a predicted distribution against a reference or prior, checking a feature’s distribution for drift over time, or implementing a VAE or RLHF penalty term.

Common mistake: treating KL divergence as an interchangeable distance metric and swapping the arguments freely. KL(P||Q) != KL(Q||P), so always be explicit about which is truth (first argument) and which is approximation (second). Separately, KL divergence is infinite wherever q is zero and p isn’t, which is the practical reason for the eps clipping above.

Procedure 5.4 (Rank Features by Mutual Information). When to use: selecting predictive features when a non-linear relationship to the label is suspected, one that a correlation matrix would miss entirely.

Common mistake: comparing mutual information scores across different runs or datasets as if they sat on a fixed, interpretable scale like a correlation’s [−1,1][-1, 1][−1,1] range. MI is non-negative, but it has no fixed scale. For a class label, its ceiling is the label’s entropy, which changes from dataset to dataset. Use it only to rank features relative to each other within the same run, not as an absolute quality threshold.

Procedure 5.5 (Diagnose a Cross-Entropy Loss That Won’t Go Down). When to use: training a classifier whose loss is stuck, exploding, or has gone to nan.

Symptom Most likely cause Fix
Loss is nan log(0) from an unclipped prediction of exactly 0 or 1 Clip predictions to [ϵ,1−ϵ][\epsilon, 1-\epsilon][ϵ,1−ϵ], or use a numerically stable framework loss instead of a manual log

It’s the natural next stop after this chapter (Murphy 2022). Christopher Olah’s essay “Visual Information Theory” (colah.github.io, 2015) is, informally, the best available visual intuition for entropy, cross-entropy, and KL divergence. It’s worth reading alongside this chapter’s equations, rather than instead of them.

5.7 How This Connects

This chapter builds on Chapter 3’s treatment of probability and distributions. Information content is only meaningful once “probability of an event” is already a settled idea. It enables the loss functions behind decision trees and neural network training (Chapter 11 and later chapters on classical and deep models). It also underpins variational autoencoders and RLHF (covered in Book 2) and the feature-selection techniques that recur throughout the data preparation chapters of this book. It is most often confused with a mix-up between entropy and cross-entropy. Entropy measures the uncertainty of the true distribution alone. Cross-entropy measures the cost of using a predicted distribution in its place. They differ by exactly the KL divergence between the two. Training a classifier means minimising cross-entropy. That indirectly minimises the KL term, since entropy is fixed.

5.9 Mental Model

Entropy is the baseline: how hard is this problem, before any model touches it? Cross-entropy is the scorecard: how well is a model doing against that baseline? KL divergence is the gap between the two: how much worse than optimal is the model being? Mutual information asks the same question of two variables instead of a model and the truth: how much does one tell you about the other? When a classifier’s cross-entropy loss converges to H(P)H(P)H(P), the model has learned everything there is to learn from the data. No amount of further training can close a gap that no longer exists.
