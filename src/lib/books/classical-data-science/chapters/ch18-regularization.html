A model reaches 98% accuracy on training data and 72% on unseen test data. That’s a textbook overfit. The model learned the training set’s noise instead of its underlying signal. Regularisation is the primary tool for fighting this. It works by penalising model complexity during training. That pushes the model towards simpler explanations that generalise better, rather than more complex ones that memorise.

18.1 Motivation

A model with enough degrees of freedom can perfectly memorise any training set. More data helps, but it often isn’t available. You need to constrain how much the model is allowed to memorise. Regularisation adds a cost for complexity to the training objective. Instead of minimising prediction error alone, the model minimises prediction error plus a penalty for large or complex weights. Penalty terms for linear models, dropout for neural networks, depth limits for decision trees, early stopping for iterative training: all forms of regularisation. The form changes by model type. The underlying principle is the same across all of them.

18.2 Exposition

18.2.1 The Bias-Variance Trade-off

Chapter 11’s Bias-Variance Trade-Off section introduced this decomposition in full: Total Error=Bias2+Variance+Noise\text{Total Error} = \text{Bias}^2 + \text{Variance} + \text{Noise}Total Error=Bias2+Variance+Noise. Regularisation trades a small increase in bias for a larger reduction in variance. The result is lower total error on unseen data.

18.2.2 Diagnosing with Learning Curves

The bias-variance trade-off is conceptual. A learning curve is how to see which side of it a model is on, before choosing whether to regularise, simplify, or collect more data. Train the model on increasing subsets of the training set (10%, 20%, and so on). At each size, record both training error and validation error, then plot both against training-set size. This is a different diagnostic from Chapter 4’s loss curve, which plots error against training epoch to check whether a model is converging. A learning curve asks a different question: would more data help?

If both curves converge to a similarly high error as training-set size grows, that’s high bias. The model is too simple for the signal in the data. No amount of extra data will help. A more flexible model or better features is the fix. If training error stays low while validation error stays higher, with a gap that persists (though usually narrows) as training-set size grows, that’s high variance. The model has the capacity to overfit. More data, or more regularisation, is the fix.

Figure 18.1 shows both failure modes side by side. On the left, LinearRegression’s training RMSE starts a touch lower, around 2.01 with the smallest training slice, then rises and settles alongside validation RMSE in a tight band, roughly 2.19 to 2.25, for the rest of the curve. Neither curve improves as more data arrives, and there’s no consistent gap between them: the signature of high bias, a model too simple to represent a sine curve no matter how much data it sees. On the right, the unconstrained decision tree’s training RMSE sits at nearly zero throughout. It memorises every training point exactly. Validation RMSE starts at 0.938, drops to around 0.71-0.75, and stays there: a gap that narrows a little, as the text above predicts, but never closes. That’s the signature of high variance.

18.2.3 L2 Regularisation (Ridge)

Ridge adds the sum of squared weights to the loss: Lossridge=Loss(y,y^)+α∑wi2\text{Loss}_{\text{ridge}} = \text{Loss}(y,\hat y) + \alpha \sum w_i^2Lossridge​=Loss(y,y^​)+α∑wi2​, where α\alphaα controls penalty strength. This pushes gradient descent towards smaller weights across the board. Ridge shrinks every weight towards zero, but keeps every feature in the model. It’s the right choice when most features are believed useful, but noisy.

18.2.4 L1 Regularisation (Lasso)

Lasso adds the sum of absolute weights instead: Losslasso=Loss(y,y^)+α∑∣wi∣\text{Loss}_{\text{lasso}} = \text{Loss}(y,\hat y) + \alpha \sum |w_i|Losslasso​=Loss(y,y^​)+α∑∣wi​∣. The geometric reason Lasso behaves differently from Ridge is subtle. The L1 penalty’s constraint region has corners exactly on the axes. When the constrained solution lands on one of those corners, a weight is driven to exactly zero. The L1 penalty has no gradient at zero, so scikit-learn fits Lasso by coordinate descent, not gradient descent. Lasso therefore produces sparse solutions. It zeros out irrelevant features, making it a form of automatic feature selection that Ridge structurally cannot do.

18.2.5 Elastic Net

Elastic Net combines both penalties: Lossenet=Loss(y,y^)+α(ρ∑∣wi∣+12(1−ρ)∑wi2)\text{Loss}_{\text{enet}} = \text{Loss}(y,\hat y) + \alpha(\rho \sum |w_i| + \tfrac{1}{2}(1-\rho)\sum w_i^2)Lossenet​=Loss(y,y^​)+α(ρ∑∣wi​∣+21​(1−ρ)∑wi2​), as scikit-learn writes it. scikit-learn also scales the data-fit term by 1/(2n)1/(2n)1/(2n) for Lasso and Elastic Net, but not for Ridge. So the same α\alphaα means a different strength in each of the three. It gets Lasso’s sparsity while handling correlated features more gracefully. Lasso tends to pick one feature from a correlated group arbitrarily and zero out the rest. Elastic Net tends to keep or drop correlated groups together instead.

18.2.6 Dropout (Neural Networks)

During each training step, dropout randomly zeroes a fraction of neurons, preventing them from co-adapting into a fixed, fragile pattern. Any given neuron has to be independently useful, not just useful as part of one specific combination. During training, frameworks such as PyTorch scale the surviving activations up by 1/(1−p)1/(1 - p)1/(1−p), where ppp is the dropout rate. That keeps the expected activation the same, so at inference every neuron is active and no scaling is needed. A dropout rate of 0.2-0.5 is typical. It’s one of the most commonly used regularisers for deep networks.

18.2.7 Early Stopping

Monitor validation loss during training. Stop once it hasn’t improved for a set number of epochs, the patience, even while training loss keeps falling. Validation loss is noisy, so a single rise isn’t enough. Patience catches the point where overfitting has set in. It’s simple, effective, and nearly free in compute terms. It’s just stopping a run early.

18.3 Trade-offs and Limits

Too much regularisation (α\alphaα too high) underfits. The model can’t capture the real signal, and both training and test performance suffer together. Too little (α\alphaα too low, or none) overfits. Training error is low but test error is high. A widening gap between the two is the real symptom. The sweet spot is found by cross-validated search over α\alphaα: RidgeCV, LassoCV, or the general hyperparameter search machinery from Chapter 17.

18.4 Procedures

Procedure 18.1 (Choose Between Ridge, Lasso, and Elastic Net). When to use: selecting a regularisation strategy for a linear model.

Quick rule: start with Ridge; try Lasso if many features are suspected irrelevant; use Elastic Net when features are highly correlated.

Procedure 18.2 (Apply Ridge with Cross-Validated Alpha). When to use: the default regularised-regression starting point.

Procedure 18.3 (Apply Lasso and Count Eliminated Features). When to use: suspected irrelevant features, or automatic feature selection is wanted alongside regularisation.

Common mistake: forgetting to scale features before regularising. An unscaled feature on a much larger numeric range gets penalised unfairly relative to a small-scale one, since the penalty is applied to the raw weight magnitude. Always StandardScaler() first.

Procedure 18.4 (Apply Regularised Logistic Regression). When to use: classification with the same overfitting risk.

Procedure 18.5 (Diagnose Bias vs. Variance with a Learning Curve). When to use: deciding whether “get more data” or “add regularisation, or a simpler model” is the right next step, before spending time or money on either.

Common mistake: reading the curve at a single training-set size instead of its overall shape. The diagnostic signal is whether the gap between the two curves narrows as size increases, not the error at any one point on its own.

18.5 Worked Example

Fitting a degree-15 polynomial to 60 noisy points generated from a simple quadratic (y=0.5x2−x+2y = 0.5x^2 - x + 2y=0.5x2−x+2 plus Gaussian noise), with a 70/30 train/test split, produces the textbook overfitting shape:

Measured: unregularised, this model reaches training RMSE 1.412 but test RMSE 1.912, about 35% worse on data it hasn’t seen. That’s a real but modest overfitting gap, given how small this dataset is (42 training points). Applying RidgeCV (best α=2.477\alpha = 2.477α=2.477) brings test RMSE down to 1.461, below even its own training RMSE of 1.819. The regularised model generalises better than it fits, the opposite of the unregularised model’s pattern. LassoCV (best α=0.076\alpha = 0.076α=0.076) does comparably well on test RMSE (1.455, the best of the three), while additionally zeroing out 11 of the 15 polynomial-feature coefficients entirely. Lasso didn’t just shrink the overfit away. It explicitly decided most of the degree-15 expansion’s terms weren’t worth keeping, leaving a much smaller model behind that generalises better than the 15-term original it started from, even without recovering the exact quadratic shape.

This is the bias-variance trade-off made concrete. Both Ridge and Lasso accept worse training fit than the unregularised model (RMSE 1.819 and 1.793 vs. 1.412), in exchange for better test performance (1.461 and 1.455 vs. 1.912). That’s a deliberate increase in bias that gets a larger reduction in variance, exactly as the Bias-Variance Trade-off section predicts.

18.9 Mental Model

Regularisation is Occam’s Razor applied to machine learning. Among models that explain the data equally well, prefer the simpler one. The penalty term is a formal, tunable statement of that preference. α\alphaα controls exactly how strongly simplicity is preferred over fit. Turn the dial too low and the model memorises noise. Turn it too high and the model can’t see the signal.
