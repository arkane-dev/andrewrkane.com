Every model we’ve met so far has assumed rows are independent and identically distributed. Shuffle them and nothing changes. A random 80/20 split is fine. Ordinary k-fold cross-validation works as-is. Time series data breaks that assumption. Today’s value depends on yesterday’s. December’s sales depend on last December’s. A random split leaks the future into training and quietly inflates every reported metric. The model gets to peek at values from after the point it’s supposed to be predicting. This chapter is the toolkit built around the thing we’ve so far ignored: order.

20.1 Motivation

A time series is a sequence of values indexed by time. Almost every real series is a mix of three components: trend (the long-run direction), seasonality (a pattern repeating at a fixed, known period), and residual noise (whatever’s left once trend and seasonality are removed). Classical decomposition splits a series into exactly these pieces, additively (y=trend+seasonal+residualy = \text{trend} + \text{seasonal} + \text{residual}y=trend+seasonal+residual), or multiplicatively when the seasonal swing grows with the level of the series. Once this is understood, the modelling task becomes clear. Forecast the trend. Forecast the seasonal pattern. Treat the residual as irreducible uncertainty.

20.2 Exposition

20.2.1 Stationarity

Most classical forecasting models (ARIMA and its relatives) assume the series is stationary. Its mean, variance, and autocorrelation don’t change over time, so the bit of history a model is fit to generalises to the next stretch. Most real series aren’t stationary on their own. They trend, and their seasonal amplitude often grows too. Differencing (subtracting each value from the one before it, yt′=yt−yt−1y_t' = y_t - y_{t-1}yt′​=yt​−yt−1​) removes a linear trend. Seasonal differencing (yt′=yt−yt−my_t' = y_t - y_{t-m}yt′​=yt​−yt−m​) removes seasonality at period mmm. The Augmented Dickey-Fuller (ADF) test checks whether differencing worked. Its null hypothesis is that the series has a unit root (is non-stationary). A small p-value (usually below 0.05) lets that null be rejected.

20.2.2 Autocorrelation and Partial Autocorrelation

Autocorrelation (ACF) at lag kkk measures how similar yty_tyt​ is to yt−ky_{t-k}yt−k​. Partial autocorrelation (PACF) measures the same relationship with the effect of all shorter lags already removed: the direct connection, not the connection mediated through intervening lags. These aren’t just diagnostics. This is how model order gets chosen. A sharp PACF cutoff after lag ppp suggests an AR(ppp) term. A sharp ACF cutoff after lag qqq suggests an MA(qqq) term. A slowly decaying ACF is the signature of non-stationarity: a signal to difference before fitting anything.

20.2.3 ARIMA: AR, I, MA

ARIMA(p,d,qp,d,qp,d,q) combines three ideas. AR(ppp), autoregressive: today’s value is a linear combination of the ppp previous values plus noise, capturing momentum. I(ddd), integrated: difference the series ddd times to make it stationary before fitting. d=1d=1d=1 handles a linear trend. d=2d=2d=2 handles a trend that’s accelerating (rare in practice). MA(qqq), moving average: today’s value is a linear combination of the qqq previous forecast errors, capturing short-lived shocks that take a few periods to wash out. SARIMA adds a second, seasonal (P,D,Q,m)(P,D,Q,m)(P,D,Q,m) block on top of the same three ideas, applied at the seasonal lag mmm instead of lag 1. Use it whenever ACF/PACF or the decomposition shows a repeating pattern the plain ARIMA terms can’t capture.

20.2.4 Exponential Smoothing

A simpler approach, from a simpler time. Or the Honey Badger of forecasting models, taking on beasts much bigger than itself. You decide! The idea is simple: forecast the next value as a weighted average of past values, with weights decaying exponentially into the past. Simple exponential smoothing handles level only. One smoothed average, nudged a little towards each new value as it arrives. Holt’s method (Holt 1957) adds a second smoothed term for trend, so the forecast can keep rising or falling instead of flattening out at the last level. Holt-Winters (Winters 1960) adds a third smoothed term on top of that, one for seasonality repeating at a fixed period. Three components, three running averages: one for level, one for trend, one for season, each updated a little every time a new value comes in. It’s cheaper to fit than ARIMA and a strong baseline. Always fit one before reaching for anything more complex.

20.2.5 Beyond Classical Statistics: ML and Deep Learning

Classical models work directly on a single series and its own history. A different, increasingly common approach treats forecasting as supervised regression. Engineer lag features (yt−1y_{t-1}yt−1​, yt−7y_{t-7}yt−7​, yt−30y_{t-30}yt−30​, …) and rolling statistics as columns, then fit a gradient-boosted tree (Chapter 12) to predict yty_tyt​ from them. This folds in external features (price, weather, promotions) that ARIMA has no natural way to use, at the cost of losing ARIMA’s built-in uncertainty intervals. Prophet sits in between: an additive trend-plus-seasonality model fit via curve-fitting rather than the ARIMA machinery, popular for its robustness to missing data and minimal tuning. Deep learning models (LSTMs, temporal convolutional networks, transformer-based forecasters) can model many related series jointly and capture long-range or nonlinear dependencies classical models can’t, at the cost of needing far more data and tuning. They’re usually overkill for a single series with a clear seasonal pattern.

20.3 Trade-offs and Limits

ARIMA is more flexible than exponential smoothing, but needs a stationary series and careful order selection. Exponential smoothing is simpler, needs less tuning, and is often just as accurate in practice. Fit both. Keep whichever generalises better on held-out data. Classical models give prediction intervals for free (well calibrated only when the model’s assumptions hold) and work with very little data. Lag-feature ML models scale better to many related series and easily incorporate external regressors, but need careful leakage-free cross-validation and don’t naturally produce calibrated uncertainty. Use multiplicative decomposition when the seasonal swing grows with the series’ level. Use additive when it stays roughly constant. This is usually obvious when plotted.

20.4 Procedures

Procedure 20.1 (Split Time Series Data Correctly). When to use: every time, never a random split or plain k-fold on time-ordered data.

Common mistake: using train_test_split(shuffle=True) or plain KFold on time-indexed data. This lets the model train on future values to predict the past, producing metrics that look great in development and fail in production.

Procedure 20.2 (Test and Achieve Stationarity). When to use: before fitting any ARIMA/SARIMA model.

Common mistake: over-differencing a series that’s already stationary. This introduces artificial oscillation and inflates variance. Re-run the ADF test after each differencing step rather than differencing “just in case.”

Procedure 20.3 (Choose ARIMA/SARIMA Orders). When to use: after confirming the differencing order needed for stationarity.

Procedure 20.4 (Evaluate a Forecast Against a Naive Baseline). When to use: after any forecast, before deciding a model is good enough to ship.

Common mistake: skipping the naive baseline. This chapter’s own Worked Example shows exactly why that’s a mistake: a fitted model can lose to “repeat the last value,” and there’s no way to know that without checking.

20.5 Worked Example

A synthetic two-year daily series (slow upward trend plus a smooth annual sine-wave seasonal component plus moderate Gaussian noise) makes stationarity and forecast evaluation concrete. The raw series fails the ADF test outright: p=0.682p = 0.682p=0.682, nowhere near significant, cannot reject non-stationarity. That’s exactly as expected given its visible trend. First-differencing fixes this clearly: p≈3×10−27p \approx 3 \times 10^{-27}p≈3×10−27, far below 0.05.

Decomposing the series confirms what it was built from: trend rising from about 109 to 127 over the two years, seasonal amplitude of roughly 16 units. The pieces are recovered correctly by construction, a useful sanity check before trusting decomposition on a real series where the ground truth isn’t known in advance.

Measured, forecasting the final 28 days: an ARIMA(2,1,2) model reaches MAE 7.228 and RMSE 8.664. The naive baseline (repeating the last training value for all 28 days) reaches MAE 4.602 and RMSE 5.713, beating the fitted ARIMA model on both metrics. This is not a mistake in the setup. It’s a real, common outcome worth understanding rather than hiding. Over a 28-day horizon against a 365-day seasonal period and a slow linear trend, the series barely moves in expectation. “Stay near the current level” is already close to the right answer. ARIMA’s extra flexibility, fitting autoregressive and moving-average structure, is mostly available to fit short-term noise correlation it has no real ability to predict out-of-sample. The model isn’t wrong to be more complex. The complexity doesn’t help at this particular horizon, on this particular series. This is exactly why Procedure 20.4 insists on the naive baseline every time. Without it, a perfectly reasonable-looking ARIMA fit with plausible-looking diagnostics would have shipped without anyone noticing it was worse than doing nothing extra at all.

20.9 Mental Model

A time series model is a weather forecaster. It doesn’t know the future. It extrapolates the patterns (trend, seasonality, momentum, mean-reversion) visible in the past. It should be honest about how much less certain it is the further out it forecasts. Stationarity is the assumption that the weather changes, but the rules generating the weather do not. The moment that assumption breaks, a regime shift, a new product launch, a pandemic, every classical forecasting model needs refitting. It was never modelling the cause, only the pattern. And as this chapter’s own Worked Example shows, sometimes the honest, humble forecast, repeat the last value, is exactly the one that should have shipped.
