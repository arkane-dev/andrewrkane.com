Before transformers, working with text felt like working with a different data type than numbers, because it was. A spreadsheet column of integers feeds into a model without a second thought. A column of customer reviews, legal clauses, or support tickets does not. The field spent decades developing techniques to bridge that gap: turn words into numbers in ways that preserve enough meaning to be useful. Understanding those techniques still matters, not as history, but because TF-IDF and classical NLP solve many enterprise text problems faster and cheaper than an LLM. The concepts here, tokens, embeddings, similarity, are exactly what’s needed to understand transformers, RAG, and fine-tuning in the chapters ahead.

27.1 Motivation

There are three progressively smarter ways to represent text as numbers. Every technique in this chapter is a variation on one of them. Bag of words counts how many times each word appears in a document, ignoring order entirely. "the cat sat on the mat" becomes {the: 2, cat: 1, sat: 1, on: 1, mat: 1}. It loses grammar and word order, which sounds disastrous, but works surprisingly well for classification tasks where word presence matters more than sequence. Its failure mode is that common words like “the”, “a”, “is” dominate every document’s vector regardless of topic. That motivates TF-IDF. Pretrained word embeddings solve a different failure mode entirely. Bag-of-words and TF-IDF both treat “car” and “automobile” as unrelated dimensions, even though they mean the same thing.

27.2 Exposition

27.2.1 TF-IDF

TF-IDF (Term Frequency–Inverse Document Frequency) is bag of words with a reweighting step. The weight of a word in a document is TF × IDF, where TF is the count of the word in that document divided by the document’s total word count, and IDF is log⁡(N/df)\log(N / \text{df})log(N/df): the log of the total document count over the number of documents containing that word. Words that appear in every document get an IDF near zero (since log⁡(N/N)=0\log(N/N) = 0log(N/N)=0). Words that appear in only a few documents get a high IDF. The net effect: common words are downweighted, rare-but-informative words are upweighted. TF-IDF is still used in production today. For topic modelling, keyword extraction, and document similarity, it’s faster, cheaper, and more interpretable than any neural approach, and Worked Example 27 below measures exactly how competitive it remains.

27.2.2 Word Embeddings and the Distributional Hypothesis

Word embeddings represent each word as a dense vector in a continuous space (typically 50–300 dimensions), trained so that words with similar meanings end up close together. The training signal comes from context: “You shall know a word by the company it keeps” (Firth, 1957). Words that appear near the same neighbours tend to mean similar things. Word2Vec (Mikolov et al., 2013) formalised this into two efficient training objectives (Skip-gram: predict context from word; CBOW: predict word from context). GloVe (Pennington et al., 2014) instead trains on a corpus-wide word co-occurrence matrix. Both produce a geometric space where vector arithmetic captures analogy: king⃗−man⃗+woman⃗≈queen⃗\vec{\text{king}} - \vec{\text{man}} + \vec{\text{woman}} \approx \vec{\text{queen}}king​−man+woman≈queen​. This isn’t magic. It’s what emerges from training on enough text that co-occurrence statistics encode relational structure. Worked Example 27 reproduces this analogy directly against a real pretrained embedding table, not as an assertion to take on faith.

27.2.3 Subword Tokenization

Word embeddings and TF-IDF both assume a fixed vocabulary, decided in advance. Every word gets its own row in a table. That breaks the moment a document contains a word the vocabulary never saw: a rare technical term, a typo, a name, a word in another language. This is the out-of-vocabulary (OOV) problem. The usual fix, a generic <unk> token, discards whatever signal that word carried.

Subword tokenization sidesteps OOV entirely by building the vocabulary out of pieces smaller than whole words. Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE) starts from individual characters and repeatedly merges the most frequent adjacent pair into a new token, thousands of times, until reaching a target vocabulary size. Common words end up as a single token (“the” stays “the”). Rare or unseen words split into recognisable pieces (“tokenization” might become “token” + “ization”). There is no OOV problem left. Any string, however unfamiliar, decomposes into some sequence of known subword pieces, worst case falling back to individual bytes or characters. WordPiece (used by BERT) and SentencePiece (used by many multilingual and modern LLM tokenizers) follow the same idea with different merge criteria. SentencePiece additionally drops the assumption that whitespace marks word boundaries, which matters for languages that don’t separate words with spaces at all.

This is the actual mechanism AutoTokenizer runs when it turns a string into token IDs (Chapter 29). It’s also why LLM pricing and context limits are measured in tokens rather than words or characters. A token is whatever the model’s subword vocabulary says it is, whole word, word fragment, or punctuation mark, all counted the same way.

Tokenization also underlies the oldest way of scoring a language model. An n-gram model estimates the probability of the next token from the previous n−1n-1n−1 tokens by counting how often that sequence appeared in a training corpus. Perplexity, the exponentiated average negative log-likelihood the model assigns to held-out text, measures how well any language model, n-gram or transformer, predicts text it wasn’t trained on. Lower perplexity means the model was less “surprised” by the real continuation. Chapter 33’s Worked Example computes this directly against a real model rather than stating it abstractly.

27.2.4 The Bridge to Transformers

Word embeddings are static. “Bank” has the same vector whether the sentence is about a river or a loan. Transformers (Chapter 29) replace this with contextual embeddings, where a word’s representation depends on its surrounding words. That’s the key advance. But the underlying idea, words as points in a semantic space where distance means similarity, is unchanged. Transformers compute it dynamically via attention instead of storing it in a static lookup table.

27.3 Trade-offs and Limits

TF-IDF is fast to compute, trivial to interpret (which features fire tells you which words mattered), needs no GPU, and runs on a laptop across millions of documents in seconds. It’s the right default for keyword search, topic classification, and spam detection. It breaks down when synonymy matters (“car” and “automobile” share no dimension), when word order changes meaning (“not good” vs. “good”), or when documents are very short. Word embeddings fix the synonymy problem and give genuine semantic similarity, which matters for retrieval and for feeding downstream neural models. But the dense vectors aren’t human-readable. General-purpose embeddings represent specialised domain vocabulary (medical, legal, code) poorly. Averaging word vectors into a document vector, the simplest way to get one, throws away word order just as thoroughly as bag of words did. Worked Example 27 shows this last trade-off is not theoretical. On a real classification task, naive embedding averaging measurably underperforms TF-IDF.

27.4 Procedures

Procedure 27.1 (Build a Text Preprocessing Pipeline). When to use: before feeding any text into a model, every time, on both training and inference paths.

Common mistake: applying preprocessing to training data but forgetting to apply the identical function to test data and new documents at inference time. Wrap it in a function and call it everywhere, never inline the steps twice.

Procedure 27.2 (Vectorise Text with TF-IDF). When to use: classification, clustering, or similarity search where bag-of-words signal is sufficient.

Key rule: always .fit_transform() on training data and .transform() (never .fit_transform()) on test data. Fitting the vocabulary/IDF table on test data leaks information about the test set into training.

Procedure 27.3 (Build a Text Classification Pipeline). When to use: bundling preprocessing, vectorisation, and a model as one fit/predict unit, so cross-validation handles the whole thing correctly.

Procedure 27.4 (Load and Query Pretrained Word Embeddings). When to use: exploring semantic relationships, or building the simplest possible document embedding.

Procedure 27.5 (Build Document Embeddings by Averaging). When to use: the simplest way to get one vector per document from word vectors.

Limitation: mean-pooling discards word order entirely, same as bag of words. For short documents the averaged signal is weak; sentence-transformers (Chapter 31) train a model specifically to produce good single-vector document embeddings rather than pooling word vectors after the fact.

27.5 Worked Example

Four categories from the 20 Newsgroups dataset, comp.graphics, rec.sport.hockey, sci.med, sci.space, give 3,949 documents total, split 75/25 stratified (2,961 train / 988 test). Two representations were built head-to-head on the identical split: TF-IDF (Procedure 27.2, unigrams+bigrams, max_features=20,000, English stopwords removed) feeding a Logistic Regression, versus 50-dimensional GloVe vectors (glove-wiki-gigaword-50, 400,000-word vocabulary) mean-pooled per document (Procedure 27.5) feeding an identical Logistic Regression.

TF-IDF scored macro-F1 = 0.9074 (91% accuracy), training and evaluating in 4.9 seconds end to end. The top TF-IDF features per class are exactly the words a human would pick by hand: comp.graphics → graphics, 3d, file, image, computer; rec.sport.hockey → game, hockey, team, games, nhl; sci.med → doctor, medical, disease, msg, pain; sci.space → space, orbit, nasa, earth, moon. This is TF-IDF’s interpretability advantage made concrete. The model’s reasoning is legible by inspecting coef_.

The mean-pooled GloVe-50 representation scored macro-F1 = 0.8327, nearly 7.5 points lower than TF-IDF, on the same split, same classifier, same evaluation. This is not a close result. It’s the opposite of what “embeddings are the more advanced technique” might lead someone to assume. On a topic-classification task where the discriminating signal is specific vocabulary (nhl, orbit, 3d), TF-IDF’s sparse, high-dimensional, exact-word-match representation preserves that signal directly, while averaging 50-dimensional word vectors blurs a document with 200 words containing three topic-defining nouns into a vector dominated by the other 197 generic words. This is Trade-offs and Limits’ warning made measurable rather than theoretical. Embeddings are not a strict upgrade over TF-IDF. They are a different tool, better suited to semantic similarity and retrieval than to topic classification with a clear, discriminating vocabulary.

27.9 Mental Model

Text → Numbers → Model. The only real question is which numbers. Bag of words uses presence and absence. TF-IDF uses importance-weighted counts. Word embeddings use learned positions in a semantic space. This chapter’s Worked Example is the reminder that “smarter representation” doesn’t mean “always better.” The right choice depends on whether the task needs exact vocabulary matching or genuine semantic similarity. Measuring both, as done here, is the only way to know which one a specific task actually needs.
