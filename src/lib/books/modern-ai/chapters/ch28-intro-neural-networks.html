Classical ML algorithms like logistic regression or SVMs require deciding in advance which features to hand them: write the edge detector, hand-craft the grammatical features, engineer the interaction terms. The model is only as good as the human intuition that shaped its inputs. Neural networks sidestep this by learning the features themselves. Give them raw inputs, pixel values, word IDs, sensor readings, and the network learns, layer by layer, what to extract. The cost is more data and more compute. The payoff is that a human stops being the bottleneck on what the model can represent.

28.1 Motivation

A neural network is a function approximator. It takes an input, applies a series of parameterised mathematical transformations, and produces an output, with the parameters (weights) adjusted during training to minimise the gap between predictions and true labels. The basic unit is the neuron: output=σ(w1x1+w2x2+⋯+wnxn+b)\text{output} = \sigma(w_1 x_1 + w_2 x_2 + \dots + w_n x_n + b)output=σ(w1​x1​+w2​x2​+⋯+wn​xn​+b), where xxx are inputs, www are learned weights, bbb is a learned bias, and σ\sigmaσ is a nonlinear activation applied after the weighted sum. A layer is a collection of neurons receiving the same inputs. A network is layers stacked in sequence, the output of one becoming the input of the next.

28.2 Exposition

28.2.1 Why Nonlinearity Matters

Without an activation function, stacking layers is pointless. A linear function of a linear function is still linear, so any depth of purely-linear layers collapses to one matrix multiplication. Nonlinear activations break this collapse. ReLU (max⁡(0,x)\max(0, x)max(0,x)) is the default for hidden layers: simple, fast to compute, and its gradient is trivial almost everywhere. Sigmoid (1/(1+e−x)1/(1+e^{-x})1/(1+e−x)) squashes to (0,1)(0,1)(0,1) for binary output layers. Softmax squashes a vector to a probability distribution for multi-class output layers.

28.2.2 Forward Pass, Loss, and Backpropagation

A forward pass computes each layer’s output sequentially: h1 = relu(W1 @ x + b1), h2 = relu(W2 @ h1 + b2), y = softmax(W3 @ h2 + b3). A loss function then quantifies how wrong the prediction was: cross-entropy for classification (−log⁡(p^true class)-\log(\hat{p}_{\text{true class}})−log(p^​true class​), small when the model is confidently right, large when confidently wrong), mean squared error for regression. Backpropagation computes how much each weight contributed to that loss, its gradient, via the chain rule applied automatically through the computation graph: ∂L/∂w=∂L/∂output×∂output/∂hidden×∂hidden/∂w\partial L/\partial w = \partial L/\partial \text{output} \times \partial \text{output}/\partial \text{hidden} \times \partial \text{hidden}/\partial w∂L/∂w=∂L/∂output×∂output/∂hidden×∂hidden/∂w. Modern frameworks compute this with one call, loss.backward(). Gradient descent then updates weights against the gradient: w←w−η ∂L/∂ww \leftarrow w - \eta \, \partial L/\partial ww←w−η∂L/∂w, in practice on small batches (mini-batch gradient descent) rather than the full dataset at once. That’s noisier per step, but far faster and often better-generalising.

28.2.3 The Universal Approximation Theorem

A network with a single hidden layer and enough neurons can approximate any continuous function to arbitrary precision. This explains why neural networks are so general, but says nothing about how many neurons are needed or how to train them well. In practice, depth is more parameter-efficient than width. That’s the entire premise behind “deep” learning.

That capacity claim is easy to over-read as “neural networks have no inductive bias.” They have close to no capacity bias. Almost nothing is off-limits to the hypothesis space. But capacity isn’t the same as what training actually finds. For a small dataset, many different functions fit the training data equally well, and gradient descent doesn’t search among them uniformly. It has a documented preference for smooth, low-complexity functions over jagged, high-complexity ones, sometimes called spectral bias. Two networks with identical capacity can still converge on different functions depending on initialisation, optimiser, and how long training runs. Architecture adds a second, stronger kind of bias on top of that. A convolutional layer is built to assume nearby pixels matter more than distant ones, a structural assumption chosen by the architect, not learned from data (Book 4, Chapter 68 covers this in depth). A plain MLP like the one in this chapter carries the weakest architectural bias of any model in this book. Almost all of its bias comes from the optimiser rather than the architecture, which is exactly why it needs more data than a model built with the right structural assumptions already in place.

28.2.4 Overfitting and Its Countermeasures

Neural networks carry enormous parameter counts. A small MLP might have tens of thousands, GPT-scale models trillions. They can memorise training data while generalising poorly. Three standard tools counter this. Dropout randomly zeroes a fraction of neurons during training (typically 20–50%), forcing the network to avoid depending on any single neuron. All neurons are active at inference, scaled accordingly. Weight decay (L2 regularisation) penalises the sum of squared weights, encouraging many small weights over a few large ones. Early stopping monitors validation loss during training and keeps the weights from the point where it was lowest, discarding whatever the model becomes afterward. Worked Example 28 below measures exactly this last effect directly.

28.3 Trade-offs and Limits

Neural networks trade the interpretability and low data requirements of classical ML for representational flexibility. No hand-crafted features, but no legible “why” behind a prediction the way a coef_ array offers, and typically an order of magnitude more training data needed before matching a simpler model’s performance. They also introduce a training-time cost simpler models don’t have: dozens to thousands of epochs of gradient descent, versus one .fit() call for logistic regression. On small, already-separable tabular data, like this chapter’s Worked Example, the flexibility that makes neural networks powerful on images and text is mostly unused. A plain logistic regression can match a neural network’s test accuracy while training in a fraction of the time.

28.5 Worked Example

Three MLPs, identical architecture (two hidden layers), trained on the Breast Cancer Wisconsin dataset (426 train / 143 test, standardised features), differing only in width and regularisation:

The overparameterised, unregularised model (73,985 parameters against 426 training examples, over 170 parameters per example) shows textbook overfitting in its validation-loss curve:

Epoch Train loss Val loss
0 0.6998 0.6224
25 0.0628 0.0832
50 0.0305 0.0747
75 0.0142 0.0798
100 0.0060 0.0877
125 0.0029 0.0976
149 (final) 0.0016 0.1054

Training loss falls monotonically toward zero for all 150 epochs, exactly as expected. But validation loss reaches its minimum of 0.0725 at epoch 43, then rises steadily for the remaining 106 epochs to 0.1054 at the end of training. This is early stopping’s justification made numeric. The weights that generalise best are not the weights training the longest would give you. They’re the weights from epoch 43, which training past that point actively makes worse on unseen data even while making training loss better.

Adding dropout (0.3) and weight decay (10−310^{-3}10−3) to the identical 73,985-parameter architecture measurably narrows this gap: minimum validation loss 0.0688 at epoch 46 (comparable to the unregularised model’s 0.0725), but the final-epoch validation loss is 0.0816, a much smaller rise from its minimum than the unregularised model’s climb from 0.0725 to 0.1054. Regularisation didn’t prevent the network from eventually drifting past its best validation point, but it slowed the drift measurably, exactly as Exposition’s description of dropout and weight decay predicts. Final test-set F1 was statistically indistinguishable across all three configurations (0.9718, 0.9718, 0.9721), including the 785-parameter small network, because Breast Cancer Wisconsin’s 30 features are cleanly separable enough that a simple model matches an overparameterised one at convergence. The overfitting story is visible in the validation-loss trajectory, not in the final headline metric. That’s exactly why monitoring the trajectory, not just the final number, is Procedure 28.2’s point.

28.9 Mental Model

A neural network is an assembly line where each station (layer) transforms data into a more useful representation, until the final station produces a prediction. Training is using calculus and repetition to figure out what each station should do. The architecture is designed, but the station-by-station transformations are learned, not hand-specified. This chapter’s Worked Example is a reminder that “learned” cuts both ways. Left running too long, the assembly line keeps learning the training data’s noise along with its signal. That’s exactly what the validation-loss curve going back up after epoch 43 shows happening in real time.
