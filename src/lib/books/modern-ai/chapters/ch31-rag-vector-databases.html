A large language model’s knowledge is frozen at training time, compressed into its weights, and impossible to verify. Ask it about a company’s internal deployment runbook, last week’s incident postmortem, or a product that launched after its training cutoff, and it either says “I don’t know,” or, worse, confidently makes something up. There are two ways to get an LLM to reason over information it wasn’t trained on: fine-tune it on that information (Chapter 34), or hand it the relevant information at request time. Fine-tuning is slow, expensive, and doesn’t solve staleness. Every data change means retraining. Handing the model the right information at request time is fast, cheap, auditable, and trivially kept current. That second approach is Retrieval-Augmented Generation. It’s very likely the first architecture an SA reaches for when a customer wants an LLM to “know about our stuff,” well before fine-tuning enters the conversation.

31.1 Motivation

RAG runs two stages at very different times. Indexing (offline, done once, updated as data changes): split documents into chunks, convert each chunk into a dense embedding, store the embeddings in a vector database indexed for fast similarity search. Retrieval and generation (online, one round-trip per query): embed the user’s query with the same embedding model, search the vector store for the closest chunks (retrieval), construct a prompt containing the retrieved chunks plus the original query (augmentation), and send that to an LLM to generate an answer grounded in the retrieved text. The model never needed to have “known” the answer. It only needed to be handed the right passage and asked to read it. Worked Example 31 below measures exactly how much this handoff matters against the alternative of not retrieving at all.

31.2 Exposition

31.2.1 Dense Embeddings vs. What Chapter 27 Covered

Chapter 27 covered TF-IDF (weighted word counts, sparse, exact-match-driven) and Word2Vec (one static vector per word). Neither is quite right for RAG. TF-IDF misses synonyms entirely. A query about “canceling a subscription” won’t match a document that says “terminate your plan,” because the words share zero vocabulary overlap. Word2Vec has no notion of a vector for a whole sentence or paragraph. RAG uses sentence embedding models (the sentence-transformers family): transformer encoders (Chapter 29) fine-tuned specifically so that semantically similar sentences land close together in vector space, regardless of exact word overlap. This is the same transformer architecture from Chapter 29, repurposed. Instead of generating text, it compresses text into a fixed-size vector.

31.2.2 Similarity Search

Once every chunk is a vector, “find the most relevant chunks” becomes “find the vectors closest to the query vector,” usually via cosine similarity: the angle between two vectors, independent of magnitude, so a long document and a short one are judged purely on directional similarity of meaning. At small scale (thousands of chunks), brute-force comparison against every stored vector is fine. At production scale (millions of chunks), vector databases use approximate nearest neighbour algorithms. HNSW (Hierarchical Navigable Small World graphs) is the most common. It trades a small amount of recall for a large speedup, turning an O(n)O(n)O(n) scan into something close to O(log⁡n)O(\log n)O(logn).

31.2.3 Chunking Strategy

Chunk size is a direct trade-off. Chunks too small (a sentence) lack surrounding context, so a retrieved chunk can be technically relevant but unintelligible alone, or the real answer splits across two chunks and neither ranks highly enough individually. Chunks too large (a whole document) turn the embedding into an average of everything the document discusses, diluting the signal for any specific question and wasting context-window space on irrelevant text. A common compromise: a few hundred tokens per chunk with 10–20% overlap between consecutive chunks, so an idea spanning a chunk boundary still appears intact in at least one chunk.

31.2.4 Failure Modes to Expect

Cosine similarity always returns something, even when nothing in the store is actually relevant. There’s no built-in “I don’t have that” signal, so a similarity threshold and an explicit “no relevant context” path are required, or the model will confidently generate an answer from irrelevant retrieved text. Irrelevant or contradictory retrieved chunks can pull generation off course even when a good chunk is also present. And empirically, LLMs pay less attention to information placed in the middle of a long context than at the very start or end (“lost in the middle”). Retrieved chunks should be ordered with the most relevant near the beginning or end of the prompt, not buried in the middle.

31.3 Trade-offs and Limits

RAG versus fine-tuning (Chapter 34): RAG wins when knowledge changes often, needs to be auditable/citable, or is too large to bake into weights economically. Fine-tuning wins when the need is to change the model’s behaviour or style rather than give it facts. RAG can hand a model a fact but can’t teach it a new skill or reasoning pattern the way fine-tuning can. Production systems often use both: fine-tuning for behaviour, RAG for facts. Self-hosted vector stores (Chroma, FAISS, self-managed pgvector) give full control with no per-query cost but mean owning uptime, scaling, and backups. Managed services (Amazon OpenSearch Service’s vector engine, Bedrock Knowledge Bases) cost more per query but remove the operational burden. That’s the right default for most production SA-recommended architectures absent a strong reason to self-host. More retrieved chunks raise the odds the right information is somewhere in context, at the cost of latency, token spend, and “lost in the middle” risk. Reranking (retrieve a larger candidate set cheaply, re-score with a slower, more accurate model, keep only the true top-k) is the standard fix.

31.4 Procedures

Procedure 31.1 (Chunk Text, Sentence-Aware). When to use: indexing any new document set, before embedding anything.

Common mistake: chunking by raw character count without regard to sentence boundaries. This regularly splits a sentence mid-thought, leaving one half’s embedding nonsensical and the other half missing context.

Procedure 31.2 (Embed Documents and Queries with the Same Model). When to use: every indexing and every query. Always the identical model and version.

Key rule: never embed queries and documents with two different models, or the same model at two different versions. Retrieval quality collapses if the query and document embeddings weren’t produced by the exact same model.

Procedure 31.3 (Set Up a Persistent Vector Store). When to use: once a document store grows past comfortable in-memory size, or persistence across restarts is needed.

Procedure 31.4 (Construct the Augmented Prompt). When to use: every generation call, after retrieval returns its top-k chunks.

31.5 Worked Example

Six short support-documentation snippets (pricing, password reset, 2FA, API limits, account termination, enterprise SLA) are indexed two ways, TF-IDF (Chapter 27’s baseline) and all-MiniLM-L6-v2 sentence embeddings (Procedure 31.2), then queried with “How do I cancel my subscription?”, a query deliberately written with zero literal word overlap against the correct document, which reads “To terminate your plan, go to Account Settings and select Close Account…”:

Doc Embedding similarity TF-IDF similarity Snippet
Termination (correct answer) 0.5147 0.0000 “To terminate your plan, go to Account Settings…”
Password reset 0.3638 0.0000 “To reset your password, click Forgot Password…”
Pricing tiers 0.1766 0.0000 “Our pricing tiers are Basic ($9/mo)…”
Enterprise SLA 0.1725 0.0000 “Enterprise customers get a dedicated…”
API rate limits 0.1549 0.0000 “The API rate limit is 100 requests…”
Two-factor auth 0.1213 0.0000 “Two-factor authentication can be enabled…”

The embedding model ranks the correct document first with a clear margin, 0.5147 versus the next-closest 0.3638, and correctly ranks it well above topically-unrelated documents like API limits or 2FA. TF-IDF, meanwhile, returns exactly 0.0000 similarity for all six documents, because the query’s words (cancel, subscription, how, my, do, i) share zero literal overlap with any document’s vocabulary, including the one document that actually answers the question. Confirming this directly: set(query_words) & set(doc0_words) returns the empty set. This is Exposition’s synonym-mismatch claim made concrete rather than asserted. TF-IDF here doesn’t retrieve the wrong document. It retrieves nothing, with every candidate tied at zero, which is exactly the “no built-in relevance signal, everything looks equally (ir)relevant” failure a naive keyword-search-only RAG pipeline would silently produce on a paraphrased query like this one.

31.7 How This Connects

This chapter builds on Chapter 27’s embedding foundations (TF-IDF and Word2Vec are the direct ancestors of the dense embeddings used here) and Chapter 29 (sentence embedding models are transformer encoders fine-tuned for this specific purpose). It enables Chapter 32’s LLM evaluation, which covers RAG-specific metrics, faithfulness to retrieved context, answer relevance, beyond generic LLM evaluation. It’s often confused with “just use a long-context model instead.” Longer context windows reduce the need for aggressive chunking but don’t eliminate RAG’s value. Retrieval still lets a system scale to a document store far larger than any context window, and stays far cheaper per query than stuffing megabytes of text into every prompt.

31.9 Mental Model

RAG turns “does the model know this?” into “can the model find this?” Finding is a search problem, not a training problem. Every RAG failure traces back to one of two places. The retrieval step returned the wrong (or no) evidence, or the generation step was given the right evidence and still didn’t use it well. This chapter’s Worked Example is a debugging lesson as much as a comparison. TF-IDF’s flat 0.0000 across every candidate is a visible, checkable symptom of a retrieval-stage failure: the kind of signal worth checking for directly before ever assuming the problem is in generation.
