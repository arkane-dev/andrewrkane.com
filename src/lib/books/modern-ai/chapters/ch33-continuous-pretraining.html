Foundation models like Llama, Mistral, and Claude are trained on enormous amounts of general internet text. They know a great deal about the world, but may know very little about a specific organisation’s world. A model trained on Common Crawl knows what a bond is in casual conversation. It may not know the precise conventions used in a firm’s credit agreements. This gap between general capability and specialist usefulness is what continuous pretraining exists to close.

33.1 Motivation

Continuous pretraining (CPT) takes an already-pretrained model and continues training it on the same objective, next-token prediction, but on domain-specific text instead of general web data. This is distinct from two adjacent techniques covered elsewhere in this book. Instruction fine-tuning (Chapter 34) teaches a model how to behave: how to respond to instructions, format answers, be helpful, changing communication style without touching what it knows. RAG (Chapter 31) gives a model access to external knowledge at inference time without touching its weights at all. CPT teaches a model what to know, baking domain knowledge directly into the weights. The analogy: CPT is residency training after medical school. The doctor already knows medicine. Residency immerses them in a specific clinical environment until its vocabulary and reasoning patterns become second nature. IFT is bedside manner training. It changes how the doctor communicates, not what they know. Worked Example 33 below measures whether that gap is actually present in a real model before assuming CPT is needed.

33.2 Exposition

33.2.1 Why the Pretraining Objective Transfers

A pretrained model learns to predict the next token, and to do this well it must develop internal representations of syntax, semantics, factual relationships, and domain conventions, entirely self-supervised, no labels required. CPT exploits this directly. Feeding a pretrained model a large corpus of domain text and continuing next-token-prediction training makes it learn the domain’s vocabulary, co-occurrence patterns, and reasoning conventions, producing a model that thinks in the domain’s language rather than one that merely knows a few domain facts.

33.2.2 When CPT Is Appropriate

CPT makes sense when a domain has characteristics poorly represented in general pretraining data: specialised vocabulary with different meanings from everyday usage, novel notation (legal citations, scientific formulae, proprietary code dialects), domain-specific reasoning patterns, or knowledge that postdates the model’s training cutoff. If the domain is already well-represented (standard Python programming, for instance), CPT is rarely worth its cost. Prompt engineering or IFT is almost always cheaper. The right way to check which situation applies is a measured perplexity comparison, not an assumption about how specialised a domain “feels.”

33.2.3 Catastrophic Forgetting

The central risk in CPT is catastrophic forgetting: continued training on domain text can degrade general capabilities as gradients optimise heavily for the new distribution. The standard mitigation is data mixing: blending domain text with general-purpose text from the original pretraining distribution, commonly 80–90% domain to 10–20% general, though the right ratio is domain-dependent and needs empirical evaluation. Other mitigations include lower learning rates (typically 10–100× lower than original pretraining), replay buffers (periodically re-including original-distribution examples), and running CPT through LoRA adapters instead of updating all weights, limiting the parameter budget that can drift.

33.2.4 Compute Requirements: FLOPs and Memory

CPT cost separates into two independent constraints. FLOPs determine how long training takes: C≈6NDC \approx 6NDC≈6ND, where NNN is parameter count (Chapter 29’s formula, N≈12⋅nlayers⋅dmodel2N \approx 12 \cdot n_{\text{layers}} \cdot d_{\text{model}}^2N≈12⋅nlayers​⋅dmodel2​) and DDD is training tokens. The 6 comes from roughly 2 FLOPs/parameter/token forward and 4 backward. Dividing CCC by a GPU’s achieved FLOPs (typically 30–50% of spec-sheet peak, due to communication and memory-bandwidth stalls) gives wall-clock time. Memory determines whether training fits at all, independent of how long it takes. Mixed-precision Adam training needs roughly 16 bytes per parameter (2 for bf16 weights, 2 for bf16 gradients, 4+4+4 for fp32 master weights and Adam’s two moment buffers) before activation memory is even counted. Three standard techniques bring this down. ZeRO sharding splits optimiser state, gradients, or weights themselves across GPUs. Gradient checkpointing recomputes activations during backward instead of storing them, at roughly 20% extra compute time. LoRA freezes the base model’s 2-bytes/param weights and trains only small low-rank adapters, dropping the trainable-portion memory from 16 bytes/param to near zero.

33.3 Trade-offs and Limits

CPT works well when a domain has genuinely specialised language absent from the original training corpus, high-quality domain text is available (10M+ tokens minimum, 1B+ preferred), multi-GPU training budget exists, and the model needs to reason in the domain rather than just answer questions about it. It breaks down on small domain corpora (the model memorises rather than generalises), well-represented domains (the base model may already suffice: Worked Example 33 shows this can be true even when intuition suggests otherwise), no GPU budget (RAG is the cheaper alternative), and cases needing behavioural change rather than knowledge. That’s IFT’s job, not CPT’s.

33.4 Procedures

Procedure 33.1 (Test Whether a Domain Gap Actually Exists). When to use: before committing any CPT budget. Fail fast if the base model is already adequate.

Procedure 33.2 (Estimate GPU Memory and Time Before Starting a Run). When to use: before requesting a training instance. Catch an out-of-memory failure on paper, not after an 8-hour job crashes at step 1.

Common mistake: sizing a run off gpu_peak_tflops instead of achieved FLOPs. Real training utilisation is typically 30–50% of the spec-sheet number, so a naive estimate using peak FLOPs underestimates wall-clock time by 2–3x.

Procedure 33.3 (Run a CPT Data Curation Pipeline). When to use: preparing any raw domain corpus for training. Never train directly on unfiltered scraped text.

Common mistake: skipping deduplication. Duplicate documents cause the model to memorise rather than generalise, and directly inflate training cost by processing the same tokens multiple times for no benefit.

33.5 Worked Example

domain_gap_test (Procedure 33.1) run against real GPT-2 small, comparing five general-domain sentences to five formal legal-contract sentences:

Text type Perplexity
General (“The weather today is sunny…”) 31.54
Legal (“Notwithstanding the foregoing, the indemnifying party…”) 26.53
Gap ratio (legal/general) 0.84x

This is not the result Motivation’s framing would predict. Per Procedure 33.1’s own thresholds, a ratio below 1.2 means “the base model handles this domain well enough.” 0.84 is not just below that threshold. It means GPT-2 finds the legal text more predictable than the everyday sentences, the opposite of the assumed direction. Per-sentence perplexities explain why. Formulaic legal phrases like “notwithstanding the foregoing” (14.1) and “the lessee hereby covenants and agrees to indemnify” (17.0) score lower perplexity than a mundane sentence like “he plays guitar every weekend with his friends at the local park” (61.2). Legal boilerplate is highly templated and repetitive: exactly the kind of low-entropy, formulaic phrasing that appears constantly across the web (contracts, terms-of-service pages, licensing text). GPT-2’s pretraining corpus likely contains a great deal of it, even though “legal language” intuitively feels specialised and rare. This is Exposition’s core warning made concrete. Intuition about which domains are “hard” for a base model is not reliable. Procedure 33.1’s measured test, not assumption, is what should gate a CPT investment decision. A domain that feels highly specialised (medical, legal, technical) may in fact be well-represented in web-scale pretraining data precisely because of how much formal/templated text of that kind exists publicly online. A domain that feels mundane can still show high perplexity if its specific phrasing is unusual enough.

Applying Exposition’s FLOPs and memory formulas to GPT-2 small itself (124,439,808 actual parameters, formula estimate 84,934,656 without the embedding matrix, consistent with Chapter 29’s Worked Example) for a hypothetical 1-million-token domain CPT run:

This gives C≈7.47×1014C \approx 7.47 \times 10^{14}C≈7.47×1014 FLOPs, full fine-tune memory of 1.99 GB, and LoRA memory of 0.27 GB: a 7.4x memory reduction for this model size, directionally consistent with (though larger than) the “3-4x” figure Exposition cites for bigger models, since the frozen-weight term (2 bytes/param) dominates LoRA’s cost more completely relative to the full 16-bytes/param figure at smaller scale.

33.7 How This Connects

This chapter builds on Chapter 29 (the architecture and parameter-count formula) and Chapter 27’s NLP fundamentals. It enables Chapter 34’s instruction fine-tuning. CPT and IFT are frequently combined in practice, CPT first for domain knowledge, IFT second for behaviour. It’s often confused with IFT. The key distinction is that CPT is self-supervised on raw text (next-token prediction, no labels), while IFT is supervised on (instruction, response) pairs: different objectives entirely, not just different data.

33.9 Mental Model

CPT is immersion: not teaching the model rules about a domain, but putting it in the domain until the domain feels native. The model already knows how to learn from text. CPT just changes what text it learns from. This chapter’s Worked Example is the reminder that immersion is only worth the cost if the model isn’t already immersed. The only reliable way to know that is to measure the gap, not to assume it exists because a domain sounds specialised.
