Instruction fine-tuning (Chapter 34) teaches a model to imitate a demonstrated response. Show it thousands of (instruction, response) pairs and it learns to reproduce that pattern. That works when there’s a single response worth demonstrating. Most of the time there isn’t. Ask for a product description and there are dozens of reasonable answers, some better than others, none uniquely “correct.” Imitation can teach a model a way to respond. It can’t teach the model to prefer better responses over worse ones when both are plausible. This chapter covers the two dominant techniques for that: Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF), and its lighter, more stable descendant, Direct Preference Optimization (DPO). Both train on comparisons rather than demonstrations.

35.1 Motivation

Reinforcement learning is a genuinely different training signal from supervised fine-tuning. SFT’s loss says “produce this exact sequence of tokens.” RL’s objective says “produce some sequence that scores well.” It doesn’t specify which one, only that it be good by some measure. That flexibility is exactly what makes RL suited to optimising qualities like helpfulness, harmlessness, and tone: qualities that are hard to demonstrate exhaustively (there’s no canonical “helpful” response to every prompt) but comparatively easy to judge. A human can reliably say which of two candidate responses is better even when neither is uniquely correct. RLHF and DPO exist to turn that comparative judgement into a training signal.

35.2 Exposition

35.2.1 A Minimal RL Vocabulary

RLHF borrows five terms from reinforcement learning, each with a direct LLM-specific meaning:

RL term General meaning Meaning for an LLM
Agent The thing making decisions The language model (the “policy”)
Environment What the agent acts on The conversation: the prompt so far
State The situation observed The tokens generated so far
Action A choice the agent makes Emitting the next token, or, more coarsely, the whole response
Reward A scalar signal for how good an action was A score for the whole response, from a reward model
Policy The agent’s state-to-action strategy The LLM’s probability distribution over next tokens: the model itself

The RL objective is to adjust the policy so that, in expectation, it earns higher reward. For an LLM this means adjusting the model’s weights so it produces responses a human, or a reward model standing in for one, rates more highly.

35.2.2 The Three-Stage RLHF Pipeline

Classic RLHF, as used for InstructGPT and the early ChatGPT models, runs in three stages, each producing an artefact the next stage consumes.

Stage 1 — Collect preference data. Show human annotators a prompt and two or more candidate completions, usually sampled from an SFT model like the one built in Chapter 34. Ask which one they prefer. The result is a dataset of (prompt, chosen, rejected) triples. Note what isn’t being collected: a “correct” answer, only a comparison.

Stage 2 — Train a reward model. A reward model is a second model, often initialised from the SFT model, with its final layer replaced by a scalar output head, trained to assign a numeric score to a (prompt, response) pair such that the chosen response scores higher than the rejected one, for every pair in the preference dataset. The standard loss is the Bradley-Terry model of pairwise comparison:

P(chosen≻rejected)=σ(r(chosen)−r(rejected))L=−log⁡σ(r(chosen)−r(rejected))P(\text{chosen} \succ \text{rejected}) = \sigma(r(\text{chosen}) - r(\text{rejected})) \qquad \mathcal{L} = -\log \sigma(r(\text{chosen}) - r(\text{rejected}))P(chosen≻rejected)=σ(r(chosen)−r(rejected))L=−logσ(r(chosen)−r(rejected))

where r(⋅)r(\cdot)r(⋅) is the reward model’s scalar output and σ\sigmaσ is the sigmoid. This loss is minimised by maximising the score gap between chosen and rejected. It never demands a specific reward value, only the correct ordering.

Stage 3 — Optimise the policy against the reward model with PPO. The SFT model is fine-tuned further. For each prompt it generates a response, the frozen reward model scores it, and Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), a policy-gradient RL algorithm, nudges the model’s weights to make higher-scoring responses more likely.

35.2.3 The KL Penalty: Why Reward Can’t Be Maximised Directly

Naively maximising reward-model score produces reward hacking. The policy finds some degenerate output the reward model over-scores, repeating a flattering phrase, stuffing responses with superlatives, that a human would rate as garbage. The reward model is a proxy for human judgement, not human judgement itself, and any proxy can be gamed if optimised hard enough.

The fix is to keep the policy close to the original SFT model while still nudging it toward higher reward, using KL divergence as a penalty term:

Objective=E[r(response)]−β⋅KL(πRL(⋅∣prompt) ∥ πSFT(⋅∣prompt))\text{Objective} = \mathbb{E}[r(\text{response})] - \beta \cdot \text{KL}(\pi_{RL}(\cdot|\text{prompt}) \,\|\, \pi_{SFT}(\cdot|\text{prompt}))Objective=E[r(response)]−β⋅KL(πRL​(⋅∣prompt)∥πSFT​(⋅∣prompt))

πRL\pi_{RL}πRL​ is the policy being trained. πSFT\pi_{SFT}πSFT​ is the frozen, original SFT model: the reference point the whole objective is anchored to. The KL term measures how far the trained policy’s output distribution has drifted from the reference model’s, and penalises the objective in exact proportion. β\betaβ controls the trade-off. Too low, and the policy is barely constrained, chasing the reward model’s blind spots until it degenerates into repetitive or nonsensical but high-scoring text. Too high, and the policy can barely move from the SFT model at all, so training accomplishes little. This is the single most consequential hyperparameter in RLHF, and it reappears with an identical role in DPO below.

35.2.4 DPO: Skipping the Reward Model and the RL Loop

PPO-based RLHF works, but it is operationally heavy: a separate reward model to train and host, online sampling from the policy during training, and a genuinely finicky RL optimiser. PPO has half a dozen sensitive hyperparameters beyond β\betaβ. Many teams found this fragile and expensive to get right.

Direct Preference Optimization makes an important observation. For the exact KL-constrained reward-maximisation objective above, the optimal policy has a closed-form relationship to the reward function. That relationship can be substituted back into the Bradley-Terry preference loss to eliminate the reward model entirely. What remains is a loss defined directly on the policy, computed directly on preference pairs, with no RL loop, no reward model, and no online sampling:

LDPO=−log⁡σ ⁣(β[log⁡πθ(chosen∣p)πref(chosen∣p)−log⁡πθ(rejected∣p)πref(rejected∣p)])\mathcal{L}_{DPO} = -\log \sigma\!\left(\beta \left[\log\frac{\pi_\theta(\text{chosen}|p)}{\pi_{ref}(\text{chosen}|p)} - \log\frac{\pi_\theta(\text{rejected}|p)}{\pi_{ref}(\text{rejected}|p)}\right]\right)LDPO​=−logσ(β[logπref​(chosen∣p)πθ​(chosen∣p)​−logπref​(rejected∣p)πθ​(rejected∣p)​])

Read it as: increase the model’s log-probability of the chosen response relative to the frozen reference model, decrease its log-probability of the rejected response relative to the same reference model, and use β\betaβ to control how strongly to do this. That’s exactly the role β\betaβ plays as the KL coefficient in PPO. DPO doesn’t add a KL penalty as a separate term. The derivation folds it directly into the loss.

DPO won broad adoption for concrete reasons: no reward model to train, store, or serve; no online generation during training (it’s ordinary supervised learning on a static preference dataset, structurally identical to SFT); far fewer hyperparameters and much more stable training; and empirically competitive results with PPO-based RLHF on most benchmarks. What’s given up is a reusable artefact. Classic RLHF’s reward model can be used to score or filter data, run best-of-N sampling, or plug into other pipelines. DPO produces only a fine-tuned policy, no standalone reward model. For most teams building a single instruction-tuned assistant, that’s a trade worth making. Teams that need a reward model as infrastructure in its own right, for LLM-judge-style automated grading (Chapter 32), or best-of-N reranking at inference time, may still find classic RLHF’s reward model earns its cost.

35.3 Trade-offs and Limits

PPO-based RLHF DPO
Infrastructure needed Reward model + RL training loop + online sampling Standard supervised training loop
Stability Notoriously sensitive to hyperparameters Much more stable
Compute cost High (reward model + generation + PPO updates) Lower (single forward/backward pass per pair, like SFT)
Reusable reward model produced? Yes No
Typical choice today Large labs with RL infrastructure and reward-model use cases beyond this one run Default choice for most preference-tuning

Preference-tuning is not a substitute for SFT. It’s the stage that comes after it. Both PPO-based RLHF and DPO need an SFT model to start from and to serve as the frozen KL reference. Skipping straight to preference-tuning on a base model that has never seen instruction data produces a policy with nothing sensible to anchor its comparisons against. And preference-tuning of either flavour can measurably degrade a model’s general capability relative to the SFT checkpoint it started from: the “alignment tax.” That may still be a worthwhile trade for a production assistant, but it should be measured (Chapter 32), not assumed away.

35.4 Procedures

Procedure 35.1 (Format Preference Data). When to use: before training a reward model or running DPO. Both need the same underlying schema.

Common mistake: mixing preference-comparison data with instruction/response SFT data (Chapter 34’s format). They train different things. SFT format has one response. Preference format has chosen and rejected for the same prompt. Keep them in separate datasets.

Procedure 35.2 (Train a Reward Model with the Bradley-Terry Loss). When to use: whenever a standalone, reusable reward model is needed: for best-of-N reranking, data filtering, or as an automated grader alongside Chapter 32’s LLM-judge techniques.

In production, trl.RewardTrainer implements this exact loss end to end. The hand-rolled version above exists to make the mechanics inspectable, not to be reproduced outside a teaching context.

Procedure 35.3 (Run DPO Directly from Its Closed-Form Loss). When to use: the default starting point for preference-tuning an SFT model, before reaching for full PPO-based RLHF.

Common mistake: setting the DPO learning rate as high as an SFT run. DPO is much more sensitive to learning rate than SFT. 5e-6 to 1e-5 is a safer starting range than the 1e-4-plus typical for LoRA SFT.

Procedure 35.4 (Run DPO at Scale with trl.DPOTrainer). When to use: once the loss mechanics are understood and a real training run is needed, typically combined with LoRA (Chapter 34) rather than full fine-tuning.

Procedure 35.5 (Choose and Diagnose the KL Coefficient beta). When to use: whenever the trained policy either barely changes from its reference or degenerates into repetitive, off-topic text.

Procedure 35.6 (Evaluate Whether Preference-Tuning Actually Helped). When to use: after any RLHF/DPO run, before shipping it.

35.5 Worked Example

The tutorial notebook builds the three pieces of preference-tuning that are small enough to run on a laptop: a reward model trained from scratch, a DPO fine-tuning loop implemented directly from its closed-form loss, and an explicit KL-divergence computation of the kind a PPO loop would use. Full PPO-based RLHF needs a GPU cluster and days of training and is out of scope for a teaching notebook; the point here is to make the losses and the KL term completely concrete rather than abstract formulas.

Reward model. Eight synthetic preference pairs were built (Listing 35.1’s schema), each pairing a helpful, complete response against a terse or evasive one for the same prompt: e.g. “The capital of France is Paris, which has been the country’s capital since 987 CE” (chosen) against “Dunno, look it up” (rejected). A reward model was built on a frozen GPT-2 backbone with a trainable 769-parameter linear head (Listing 35.2), and trained for 30 epochs with the Bradley-Terry loss. The average loss fell from 0.575 at epoch 0 to 0.015 at epoch 29, a roughly 38x reduction. After training, the reward model correctly ranked all 8 of 8 preference pairs, assigning every chosen response a higher score than its paired rejected response (e.g. r(chosen) = -8.839 vs. r(rejected) = -12.693 for the France question; r(chosen) = -6.921 vs. r(rejected) = -12.460 for the hash-table question). The loss only ever pushed the ordering apart, never toward a specific value, which is exactly why all eight scores landed in a similar negative range yet still separated cleanly within each pair.

DPO. The same eight preference pairs were used to fine-tune GPT-2 directly with Listing 35.3, at β=0.1\beta = 0.1β=0.1, for 40 steps, cycling through the eight pairs five times. At step 0 the policy and reference model were identical, so the preference margin (chosen log-probability minus rejected log-probability, relative to the frozen reference) started at exactly +0.0000 and the DPO loss started at 0.6931: −log⁡σ(0)-\log \sigma(0)−logσ(0), the loss’s value when the policy has no preference at all. By step 10 the margin had jumped to +19.53 and the loss had fallen to 0.133. By the final step the margin reached +40.72, a clear and monotonic move in the correct direction, with loss falling to 0.025-0.027 over the last several logged steps. The margin is exactly the quantity DPO’s loss pushes upward: the policy’s preference for the chosen response over the rejected one, measured relative to the frozen reference, increasing without a reward model or a single RL rollout involved.

KL penalty, made concrete. For the prompt “What is the capital of France?”, a single forward pass computed KL(πpolicy ∥ πreference)\text{KL}(\pi_{\text{policy}} \,\|\, \pi_{\text{reference}})KL(πpolicy​∥πreference​) over the next-token distribution using Module 05’s formula, ∑xp(x)[log⁡p(x)−log⁡q(x)]\sum_x p(x)[\log p(x) - \log q(x)]∑x​p(x)[logp(x)−logq(x)]. The result was 0.201 nats. In a real PPO loop this exact quantity is computed at every generated token, summed over the whole response, and subtracted (scaled by β\betaβ) from the reward-model score before the policy-gradient update: the concrete mechanism behind the abstract “Objective=E[reward]−β⋅KL(⋅)\text{Objective} = \mathbb{E}[\text{reward}] - \beta \cdot \text{KL}(\cdot)Objective=E[reward]−β⋅KL(⋅)” formula above.

35.7 History and Further Reading

Murphy’s Advanced text gives the formal reinforcement-learning foundations, policies, value functions, policy gradients, that PPO builds on (Murphy 2023). “InstructGPT” (Ouyang et al.) is the paper that established the three-stage RLHF pipeline this chapter’s Exposition follows (Ouyang et al. 2022). “Direct Preference Optimization” (Rafailov et al.) is the DPO paper. Its closed-form derivation is more approachable on a read than its reputation suggests (Rafailov et al. 2023). “Proximal Policy Optimization” (Schulman et al.) is the original PPO paper, for the RL side of classic RLHF (Schulman et al. 2017). Burkov’s Hundred-Page Language Models Book covers RLHF alongside Constitutional AI as a contrasting alignment approach, worth a look for how the two differ in how they incorporate human oversight (Burkov 2025). The Hugging Face trl documentation is worth keeping open alongside this chapter for runnable implementations of RewardTrainer, DPOTrainer, and PPOTrainer.

35.8 How This Connects

This chapter builds directly on Chapter 34. The SFT model from that chapter is both the starting point for the policy being trained here and the frozen reference model anchoring the KL/DPO term. It is not a replacement for instruction fine-tuning. It is the stage that comes after it. It also depends on the KL divergence introduced early in this book’s information-theory material. The same formula that measures how far two probability distributions have drifted apart is, here, the mechanism that keeps preference-tuning from collapsing into reward hacking, whether applied as PPO’s explicit penalty or folded implicitly into DPO’s loss. It connects forward to Chapter 32: a reward model is itself a learned evaluator, trained the same way an LLM-judge is prompted, to produce a quality signal from a comparison. And it can develop the same biases (length, style, sycophancy) an LLM-judge can. It is often confused with instruction fine-tuning. The distinction that matters is that SFT teaches a model a correct behaviour via imitation of demonstrations, while RLHF and DPO teach a model to prefer better behaviour via comparison, without ever specifying a single correct response.

35.10 Mental Model

SFT is apprenticeship: watch an expert, imitate them. RLHF and DPO are coaching. Rather than handing the model a script, show it two attempts and say “that one was better,” repeatedly, until it internalises the underlying preference. The KL term, whether PPO’s explicit penalty or DPO’s implicit one, is the leash that stops the model wandering off to please the coach’s proxy metric instead of the coach’s actual intent.
