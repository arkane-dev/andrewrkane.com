“This is slow” is a feeling, not an engineering statement. Performance engineering starts by turning that feeling into a number, then a location (which specific line, function, or resource is actually responsible), and only then a fix. Skipping straight to “I think the bottleneck is X, let me rewrite X” is the single most common and most expensive mistake in this discipline. It is entirely possible to spend a week optimizing code that was never the bottleneck while the actual slow part sits untouched. This chapter establishes the two disciplines the rest of this book depends on: benchmarking correctly, and knowing in advance, via Amdahl’s Law, the theoretical ceiling on what any given optimization effort can possibly achieve.

48.1 Motivation

Two ideas anchor everything else in this book. First, correct benchmarking is harder than it looks, and getting it wrong produces confidently wrong conclusions. A single measurement of almost anything on a real computer can be dominated by one-time setup costs that have nothing to do with steady-state performance. Second, Amdahl’s Law tells you, before writing a line of optimization code, the absolute ceiling on how much a given fix can possibly help, so a genuinely unfixable bottleneck can be told apart from one that simply hasn’t had the right fix applied yet. Together these two ideas form the discipline every later chapter assumes: measure correctly, and know what you’re measuring against before committing real effort.

48.2 Exposition

48.2.1 The Single-Measurement Trap

A small NumPy matrix multiplication (200×200 @ 200×200), timed once with a naive time.perf_counter() call, took 15.261 ms†. Timing the identical operation 200 times and looking at the distribution tells a very different story:

Statistic Time
Single naive measurement 15.261 ms
Repeated (200x): minimum 1.147 ms
Repeated (200x): median 1.178 ms
Repeated (200x): mean 1.221 ms
Repeated (200x): maximum 4.521 ms

The single naive measurement was, in fact, close to the single slowest of all 200 repetitions, a 13.0x overestimate of the operation’s real steady-state cost. The cause: the first call pays for NumPy/BLAS’s one-time initialization (thread-pool setup, library lazy-loading), a cost that has nothing to do with the operation’s steady-state speed. This is the same class of trap as the GPU-kernel material’s “first CUDA kernel launch pays for context initialization” lesson (Chapter 39), a general principle, not a library-specific quirk: the first measurement of almost anything on a computer includes setup costs that a single naive timing cannot distinguish from the real cost.

The fix is always the same shape: warm up (run the operation once or several times, discard those runs), then measure many repetitions, and report a robust statistic: minimum or median, never the mean alone. A few unlucky OS scheduling interruptions can badly skew a mean upward, while the minimum reflects best-case, most-representative-of-steady-state performance, and the median reflects typical real-world cost including some (but not extreme) noise.

48.2.2 Amdahl’s Law: The Ceiling You Can Compute in Advance

If a fraction sss of a program’s time is strictly serial (it cannot be parallelized no matter how many workers are thrown at it) and the remaining fraction p=1−sp = 1 - sp=1−s is perfectly parallelizable, the maximum possible speedup from NNN workers is:

Speedup(N)=1s+p/N\text{Speedup}(N) = \frac{1}{s + p/N}Speedup(N)=s+p/N1​

As N→∞N \to \inftyN→∞, this approaches 1/s1/s1/s, a hard ceiling set entirely by the serial fraction, no matter how much parallel hardware is added. A program that is 90% parallelizable (s=0.1s = 0.1s=0.1) can never go faster than 10x, even with infinite cores.

A workload with a fixed 0.3-second serial setup cost plus a parallelizable chunk of CPU work, run across 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, and 32 worker processes on this book’s 16-core/32-thread reference rig, gives a measured serial fraction of s≈0.110s \approx 0.110s≈0.110, p≈0.890p \approx 0.890p≈0.890:

Workers Amdahl prediction Measured speedup Gap
1 1.00x 1.00x 0.00
2 1.80x 1.89x -0.08
4 3.01x 3.26x -0.26
8 4.52x 4.60x -0.08
16 (= physical core count) 6.03x 5.47x 0.56
32 (= hyperthread count) 7.25x 4.81x 2.44

At low worker counts the measured speedup tracks Amdahl’s prediction closely, in this run, even slightly ahead of it, well within the noise of process-scheduling variance. Past the physical core count (16), a real and growing shortfall opens up: at 32 workers, measured speedup falls 2.44x short of the idealized prediction. This gap is itself an important lesson, not measurement error. Amdahl’s Law assumes zero overhead from parallelizing: no process-spawn cost, no inter-process communication, no resource contention. Real multiprocessing has all of these, and the gap widens sharply once execution moves from the 16 physical cores into the 16 additional hyperthreads (17-32), which do not provide genuine additional execution resources for CPU-bound integer work the way physical cores do.

The practical use of Amdahl’s Law isn’t the exact number. It’s the sanity check. Before spending a week parallelizing a workload, estimate its serial fraction and compute the ceiling. If the ceiling is 3x and the goal is 20x, parallelizing harder is the wrong plan. The serial fraction itself needs to shrink, or the total work needs to shrink, or different hardware is needed entirely, exactly the question Chapter 55 answers for GPU offload.

48.3 Trade-offs and Limits

Rigorous benchmarking, warm-up, repetition, robust statistics, is worth its overhead any time a number will inform a real decision: buying more hardware, rewriting a function, confirming an optimization is even real. It is especially necessary when comparing two implementations, since a single-shot comparison can easily have its conclusion flipped by measurement noise alone. A quick, rough single time.perf_counter() measurement is fine for a first “is this obviously way too slow” gut check before investing in proper measurement, but never for a comparison between alternatives, and never for a number that will be reported to someone else.

Amdahl’s Law is a ceiling, not a forecast. It is the wrong tool for estimating exact production throughput (real overhead pulls measured results below the prediction, as seen above) and it says nothing about how to reduce the serial fraction. It only says that reducing it, rather than adding workers, is the lever worth pulling once the ceiling has been hit. It also assumes fixed problem size. Gustafson’s law, not covered in depth here, addresses the alternative regime of scaling the problem size along with the hardware.

48.4 Procedures

Procedure 48.1 (Benchmark a Function Correctly). When to use: any time a trustworthy timing number is needed, before comparing two implementations, before claiming an optimization worked.

warmup (3-10 calls, more for anything doing lazy initialization such as NumPy or a first database connection) discards calls before timing starts. repeat (50-200 for microbenchmarks, fewer for expensive operations) sets how many timed repetitions to collect. Report the minimum for “what’s the best this can do” and the median for “what does this typically cost in practice.” Common mistake: reporting a single time.perf_counter() measurement as “the” speed of an operation. This chapter’s own single-measurement trap shows this can overstate cost by more than 10x for any operation with first-call setup cost.

Procedure 48.2 (Estimate Amdahl’s Ceiling Before Optimizing). When to use: before investing time parallelizing a workload. Know the theoretical maximum being worked toward.

Common mistake: estimating the serial fraction from intuition (“most of this should parallelize fine”) rather than actually profiling it (Chapter 50). A serial fraction that “feels like” 5% is very often actually 15-20% once measured. File I/O, a final aggregation step, or a shared-resource lock are common, easy-to-underestimate serial costs.

Procedure 48.3 (Empirically Verify Amdahl’s Law on a Real Workload). When to use: confirming a real workload’s parallel scaling matches, or falls short of, theory, before committing to a specific worker count in production.

Common mistake: testing only up to the machine’s physical core count and extrapolating linearly beyond it. This chapter’s own measurement shows the speedup curve bending noticeably once execution crosses from physical cores into hyperthreads (16 into 32 on the reference rig). Hyperthreads help I/O-bound or mixed workloads more than pure CPU-bound integer work, and assuming linear scaling there overestimates a production deployment’s real throughput.

48.6 History and Further Reading

Amdahl’s original paper, “Validity of the Single Processor Approach to Achieving Large Scale Computing Capabilities” (Amdahl 1967), is three pages and worth reading directly. The formula this chapter builds on appears there in essentially its modern form. Gustafson’s “Reevaluating Amdahl’s Law” (Gustafson 1988) is the standard counter-perspective, addressing the regime where problem size scales along with available hardware rather than staying fixed, which this chapter does not cover in depth. The Python timeit module’s own documentation is worth reading for its guidance on avoiding common benchmarking mistakes beyond what this chapter covers. Fregly’s AI Systems Performance Engineering (Fregly 2026), Chapter 1, applies this same profile-before-you-optimise discipline at GPU cluster scale, where the analogous measurement is “goodput,” the share of a cluster’s theoretical peak throughput actually spent on useful work, rather than raw FLOPS or device utilisation, worth reading once this chapter’s single-measurement trap has been felt directly. Cautaerts and Ghorbanfekr’s GPU-Accelerated Computing with Python 3 and CUDA (Cautaerts and Ghorbanfekr 2026), Chapter 1, works through the identical Amdahl’s-law speedup-estimation exercise this chapter opens with, then measures the actual, empirically-realised benefit of parallelisation against that estimate on a real workload, the same estimate-then-verify discipline this chapter argues for.

48.9 Mental Model

A stopwatch held by an unsteady hand, clicked once, tells you almost nothing. A hundred clicks, with the obviously-fumbled first one discarded, tells you the truth. Amdahl’s Law is the physics lecture sat through before the race even starts, telling you the fastest possible time no amount of training can beat, given how much of the course is uphill (serial) versus flat (parallelizable).
